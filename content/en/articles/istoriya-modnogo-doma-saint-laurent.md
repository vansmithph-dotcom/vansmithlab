Publication passport

Working title

The history of the Saint Laurent fashion house: from Le Smoking and Rive Gauche to Hedi Slimane, Anthony Vaccarello and Saint Laurent Productions

Editorial thesis

Saint Laurent's influence rests not on a list of "firsts" but on the creation of a new relationship between haute couture and modern life. Yves Saint Laurent consistently translated men's clothing, uniform, street types, painting, theatre, cinema and non-European visual sources into the women's wardrobe. Pierre Bergé turned this practice into a durable organisation and a separate ready-to-wear system. After 1999 the house survived as part of a large luxury group: its later history became a test of whether an authorial legacy can be continued without the author himself.

Reader question

How did a house founded by Yves Saint Laurent and Pierre Bergé in 1961 change women's clothing, designer ready-to-wear and the visual culture of fashion, and then survive the founder's departure, a change of owners and a series of radically different creative periods?

Editorial promise

To show Saint Laurent as a system of clothing, images, business, archives and cultural institutions. The article separates the history of Yves Saint Laurent haute couture from the operating commercial house Saint Laurent, does not repeat advertising claims of "primacy" without qualification, and treats freedom, gender, cultural borrowing and sustainability as a subject of analysis rather than ready-made brand slogans.

Material boundaries

The primary object is the fashion house founded in 1961, including the haute couture of 1961–2002, Saint Laurent Rive Gauche, the contemporary ready-to-wear, leather goods, footwear, accessories and cultural projects.

Yves Saint Laurent's biography before 1961 is considered only as necessary context: Oran, training in Paris, work at Dior, the meeting with Pierre Bergé and the departure from the house of Dior.

YSL Beauté after the 2008 deal is treated as a licensed business owned by L'Oréal, not as a fashion division run by Saint Laurent.

The Musée Yves Saint Laurent Paris, the Fondation Pierre Bergé – Yves Saint Laurent and the Musée Yves Saint Laurent Marrakech are linked to the preservation of the heritage but are not organisationally identical to the operating commercial house.

Formulations such as "invented the women's tuxedo", "liberated women" and "created ready-to-wear" are replaced with more precise ones: popularised, institutionalised, adapted, created a separate authorial line or changed the professional model.

Cultural references to Africa, Morocco, Russia, Spain, India and China are considered in their historical context and with the contemporary question of the power of interpretation; aesthetic admiration does not cancel an analysis of the colonial gaze.

Current financial and management data are fixed as of 5 August 2026 and are not carried over to other periods.

Short answer

The house of Yves Saint Laurent was founded in Paris in 1961 by Yves Saint Laurent and Pierre Bergé. It entered history through the systematic transfer of male forms into the women's wardrobe, a dialogue with art and cinema, and the launch of Saint Laurent Rive Gauche — an autonomous designer ready-to-wear line. After the sale of the group and the founder's retirement in 2002, the brand was developed by Tom Ford, Stefano Pilati, Hedi Slimane and Anthony Vaccarello. Today Saint Laurent belongs to Kering, is run by CEO Cédric Charbit and maintains its cultural influence through clothing, leather goods, Rive Droite, book spaces and its own film production. [1][7][21][25][27]

The history of the Saint Laurent fashion house

One sentence: Saint Laurent made modernity not the theme of a single collection but a principle of the organisation of a fashion house — from the male construction and ready-to-wear to authorial photography, cinema and cultural spaces.

1. Before 1961: Oran, Dior and the shaping of Saint Laurent's language

Yves Henri Donat Mathieu-Saint-Laurent was born in 1936 in Oran, in French Algeria. A childhood between the Mediterranean, the family interior, theatre and magazines later became part of the designer's authorial mythology, but historically what matters more is something else: he early on perceived clothing not as an isolated object but as a stage where body, role, set and character meet.

In 1955 Saint Laurent began working for Christian Dior. After Dior's death in 1957 the twenty-one-year-old designer took over the house and presented the Trapèze collection in January 1958. It preserved the mastery of couture but loosened the emphasised waist of the New Look and gave the body more movement. The period at Dior showed the future method: Saint Laurent did not destroy the system with a single gesture but changed its internal proportions. [2][31]

In 1960 a collection inspired by beatnik culture, leather jackets and the street met the resistance of the conservative couture milieu. After military service, illness and dismissal from Dior, Saint Laurent, together with Pierre Bergé, began to build an independent house. This prehistory explains why the new company was built from the start not only as an atelier but as a defence of creative autonomy.

2. 1961: the founding of the house and the designer's alliance with Pierre Bergé

On 4 December 1961 the Yves Saint Laurent haute couture house opened at 30 bis rue Spontini in Paris. The founders and effective architects of the project were Yves Saint Laurent and Pierre Bergé. If Saint Laurent created the silhouettes, Bergé provided the financing, the negotiations, production discipline, legal protection and the long-term structure of the company. [1][2]

The house's history is often retold as the biography of a genius, yet without Bergé it is incomplete. Their partnership created a model in which the fragility of the creative process was compensated by a rigid organisational structure. The house had to release collections, serve clients, obtain licences, expand categories and yet remain a space for authorial risk.

The name Yves Saint Laurent at once denoted a person, a couture house and a commercial sign. Later this coincidence would become a source of complexity: after the designer's departure the name would continue to exist as a brand, while the archive of his personal work would pass to a separate cultural institution.

3. 1962: the first collection, the pea coat and the trench between couture and the street

The house's first collection was presented on 29 January 1962 in the salons of rue Spontini. The museum archive notes the international attention given to the return of the young couturier. The opening image was a pea coat with white trousers — an object drawn from naval and men's functional clothing rather than from the traditional vocabulary of evening couture. [3][4]

A trench was present in the same collection. The pea coat and the trench matter not as Saint Laurent's inventions but as an early signal of his method: an object with an already existing social biography is carried into a new wardrobe, cleaned, proportionally rebuilt and given a couture execution.

Thus the house began to form a contemporary basic vocabulary: coat, jacket, trousers, blouse and dress had not only to create a seasonal effect but to return in altered versions. Unlike a logic of constant replacement, Saint Laurent developed the idea of style as a set of stable instruments.

4. Cassandre and YSL: the logo as compact architecture of a name

In 1961 the French graphic designer Adolphe Jean-Marie Mouron, known as Cassandre, created the interlaced monogram sign YSL. The vertical composition links three letters so that they read at once as initials and as a single calligraphic construction. The house's official archive continues to use this sign as part of its visual heritage. [1][2]

The monogram reflects an important contradiction of Saint Laurent: geometric discipline is joined with a sensual line. The letters do not sit side by side but cross one another, creating a sense of movement and control. The sign transfers easily onto packaging, hardware, cosmetics, print advertising and store architecture.

After 2012 the ready-to-wear was renamed Saint Laurent, yet YSL did not disappear. The monogram survived in the trademark and cultural system, and the full name continued to live in museums, the archive, the beauty licence and legal contexts. It is therefore correct to distinguish: Yves Saint Laurent as the historical designer and haute couture house; Saint Laurent as contemporary brand communication; YSL as a stable sign.

5. 1965: Mondrian — not a print of a painting but a translation of painting into construction

In autumn 1965 Saint Laurent presented a collection with dresses dedicated to Piet Mondrian. The best-known models were built as straight wool forms in which the black lines and colour planes were not a surface print but constructively assembled elements. The museum archive indicates that twenty-six of the 106 sketches in the collection referred to the artist's work. [5]

The strength of the dress lies in the correspondence of form and source. The flat plane of the shift dress allowed the body to become a moving carrier of an abstract composition, but the seams, the mass of fabric and the curve of the figure made the result different from a canvas. The Met considers this work an example of how the flatness of a dress became a field for colour blocks. [29]

The Mondrian dress is often used as proof of an alliance between fashion and art. It is more accurate to speak of a translation between media: painting provided a system of relations, and couture solved the problems of cut, seam, fit and movement. This approach later extended to Matisse, Picasso, Van Gogh, Cocteau and other authors, though not all homages were equally direct or legally arranged.

6. 1966: Le Smoking and the new social role of the women's suit

In the autumn-winter 1966 haute couture collection Saint Laurent presented Le Smoking — a women's version of the evening tuxedo. He used male codes — lapels, satin, the white shirt, trousers, a cummerbund — but adapted the proportions to the female body. Couture clients received the image cautiously; the museum notes that the first version sold in only one copy, while the Rive Gauche version became a commercial success. [6]

Le Smoking was not the first case of a woman in men's evening dress. Marlene Dietrich, the garçonne culture and queer milieus had used the suit and tuxedo long before 1966. Saint Laurent's significance lies elsewhere: he carried this image into the institution of Paris couture and then made it a repeatable part of designer ready-to-wear. [32][33]

The tuxedo changed more than appearance. It offered the woman an alternative to the evening dress and allowed her to occupy space through the shoulder line, the vertical of the trousers and a controlled closedness. But the formula "Saint Laurent liberated women" oversimplifies the history: the designer created an influential tool, and its social meaning arose thanks to the women who chose, wore and redefined the suit.

7. Rive Gauche: designer ready-to-wear as a separate system

On 26 September 1966 the Saint Laurent Rive Gauche boutique opened on rue de Tournon. The Musée Yves Saint Laurent characterises Saint Laurent as the first couturier to open a separate ready-to-wear boutique under his own name. The main innovation lay not in the existence of ready-to-wear as such but in the attitude to it: Rive Gauche was not a cheaper copy of couture but received its own models, prototypes, graphics, music, interior and a young audience. [7]

The Left Bank of Paris denoted a literary, student and avant-garde milieu. The name turned geography into a position: a fashion house could address not only the closed circle of couture clients but also working women, the young and an international urban public. In 1968 a boutique opened in New York, in 1969 in London; around the same time a men's direction appeared.

Rive Gauche helped establish designer ready-to-wear as an autonomous sector between mass clothing and couture. This model changed the economics of fashion houses: an authorial image could be reproduced more widely, faster and across more categories without abandoning the symbolic capital of high fashion.

8. 1967–1969: the trouser suit, safari, jumpsuit and transparency

In 1967 Saint Laurent presented the house's first trouser suit. In 1968 the safari jacket drew wide attention, and a jumpsuit appeared in the collection. These objects came from men's business clothing, military and colonial uniform, aviation and labour. Saint Laurent did not hide the origin of the forms but changed their fit and context. [8][9]

The Met stresses that women had worn trousers earlier — for sport, at home, on stage or as a demonstrative exception. What was new was the turning of the trouser suit into an acceptable urban ensemble for the fashionable woman. The house's contribution should therefore be described as an enlargement of the normal wardrobe, not a sudden creation of a form from nothing. [33]

At the same time Saint Laurent worked with transparency and exposure. A transparent blouse could be combined with a strict jacket, turning control and vulnerability into a single composition. The house's visual language was built on a tension: the male structure and the open skin, the practical object and the erotic staging, the uniform and individuality.

9. Cinema, muses and the collective authorship of the Saint Laurent image

Cinema was for the house not a late marketing platform but part of its early practice. In 1967 Saint Laurent created the wardrobe of Catherine Deneuve for Luis Buñuel's film Belle de Jour. The museum notes that the film's popularity heightened demand for trench coats and footwear linked to the heroine's image. [10]

Betty Catroux, Loulou de La Falaise, Paloma Picasso, Catherine Deneuve, Zizi Jeanmaire and other women were not merely passive mannequins. Their ways of wearing vintage, men's clothing, jewellery and evening pieces became a research environment for the house. Loulou de La Falaise later worked on accessories; Anne-Marie Muñoz was a key studio collaborator; the atelier turned a sketch into a material object.

The personal cult of the author can hide the collective nature of fashion design. The history of Saint Laurent must include not only the couturier and the CEO but also the studio, the première d'atelier, the models, the photographers, the artists, the actresses and the clients. The brand itself was built as a network of repeating relationships between clothing and the people who gave it a public life.

10. Africa, Morocco and the cultural geography of inspiration

The spring-summer 1967 collection turned to African materials and forms: wooden beads, raffia, straw, gold thread and Bambara sculpture. The museum describes it as an attempt to return craft techniques at a moment of growing industrial production. At the same time a contemporary analysis must ask: who names the object, who receives the authorship, and how a European couture house turns a cultural form into its own capital. [9]

In 1966 Saint Laurent and Bergé visited Marrakech for the first time. The designer returned there regularly and linked the city to his understanding of colour. Morocco became a real place of life, work, collecting and garden patronage, but in the fashion narrative it also turned into a resource of exotic imagination. [11]

Cultural influence should not be automatically called appropriation or dialogue. Concrete questions are needed: was the source named; did the bearers of the tradition take part; what materials and techniques were used; who received the economic and symbolic benefit; and how the image related to the colonial history of France and North Africa. Such an approach lets the aesthetic complexity be preserved without romanticising inequality.

11. 1971: the Libération collection and fashion's right to an uncomfortable memory

On 29 January 1971 the Libération collection was presented, also known as Quarante or the Scandal collection. Short dresses, platforms, heavy make-up, fur and emphasised shoulders referred to Paris fashion of the 1940s and the images of Paloma Picasso in flea-market clothes. The press sharply criticised the collection, since the memory of the occupation remained painful. [12]

Saint Laurent did not documentarily reconstruct a military uniform. He used an already secondary urban style in which shortage, improvisation, sexuality and bad taste were mixed with postwar nostalgia. The collection posed the question of temporal distance: when does a recent trauma become available for a fashion quotation?

Its influence showed not in immediate approval but in the spread of the retro silhouette. This episode shows that fashion history does not move only through beautiful innovations. Sometimes a new norm arises from an image that at the moment of its appearance seems morally dubious, too close or aesthetically wrong.

12. 1974–1977: avenue Marceau, Ballets Russes, China and Opium

In 1974 the haute couture house moved to the hôtel particulier at 5 avenue Marceau. Saint Laurent worked there for almost thirty years; today the Musée Yves Saint Laurent Paris is located in the building. The physical space matters as a place of production, presentation and preservation, not only as a beautiful historical interior. [13]

In July 1976 the house presented a collection inspired by the Ballets Russes. Rich colours, fur, velvet, embroidery, peasant blouses and theatrical volumes showed another side of Saint Laurent: beside the black tuxedo existed a decorative, historical and stage luxury. The house's official history links this show to a new level of runway theatricalisation. [1][34]

In 1977 the Les Chinoises collection coincided with the launch of the Opium fragrance. The name, the visual campaign and the romanticisation of the "Orient" became commercially powerful but require a critical reading: the drug, colonial trade and a generalised "Asian" aesthetic were transformed into a luxury fantasy. [14]

These collections show the range of the house: the modern wardrobe and the historical spectacle did not exclude one another. Saint Laurent could build influence through the minimal men's suit and through maximal decoration — provided the silhouette remained legible.

13. The 1980s: museum recognition and the designer's turn into a cultural institution

In December 1983 The Metropolitan Museum of Art opened the exhibition Yves Saint Laurent: 25 Years of Design. Later Met materials call it the first monographic show of a living designer in the Costume Institute. This event strengthened the idea that contemporary fashion could be a subject of museum canonisation within the author's lifetime. [15][30]

Museum recognition did more than confirm Saint Laurent's success. It changed the status of the fashion archive: collection, sketch, photograph and runway image began to be treated as cultural documents. At the same time the exhibition helped fix a set of "icons" — Mondrian, Smoking, safari, Ballets Russes — that later began to govern the memory of the house.

In the 1980s Saint Laurent continued to create artist homages and complex evening ensembles, but the system was already becoming larger than a single creative season. Licences, perfumery, international retail and stock-market mechanisms turned the authorial name into a financial asset. This prepared the future conflict between the personal legacy and corporate ownership.

14. 1989–1999: the stock exchange, Sanofi and the sale to the Gucci Group

In 1989 Groupe Yves Saint Laurent was listed on the stock exchange. In 1993 the company was sold to Elf-Sanofi. These steps showed that the house was already not only a couture atelier but a group including ready-to-wear, fragrances, cosmetics, licences and international distribution. [2]

In 1999 Sanofi sold Yves Saint Laurent to the Gucci group, which belonged to Pinault-Printemps-Redoute, later renamed Kering. Yves Saint Laurent and Pierre Bergé retained control of haute couture, while ready-to-wear and beauty moved into the corporate system of the Gucci Group. [16]

This division created two temporal rhythms within one name. Couture continued to follow the rhythm and authority of the founder, while ready-to-wear had to grow quickly and compete on the global luxury market. Subsequent creative directors no longer worked in the independent house of 1961 but in a structure where a collection was part of a large portfolio strategy.

15. 1997–2002: Elbaz, Slimane, Tom Ford and the closing of haute couture

In 1997 Saint Laurent appointed Alber Elbaz to lead the women's Rive Gauche line and Hedi Slimane the men's. The official history treats this move as an attempt to prepare a continuation of the house after the founder's departure. The period was short: after the Gucci Group acquisition creative control of ready-to-wear passed to Tom Ford. [1]

Ford brought visual sexuality, a rigid commercial focus and a language of global advertising already proven at Gucci. His Saint Laurent was not a literal continuation of couture; he used the historical codes as material for a new luxury business of the early 2000s.

On 7 January 2002 Yves Saint Laurent announced the end of his career. On 22 January a retrospective show was held at the Centre Pompidou, and in October the haute couture division officially closed. This moment cannot be described as the end of the house: the authorial couture line ended, but the ready-to-wear brand continued within the group. [17][18]

The closure also created an institutional fork. The archive of 1962–2002 became the basis of the Fondation Pierre Bergé – Yves Saint Laurent, while the commercial Saint Laurent had to produce new collections without access to the founder's living authorial decision.

16. 2004–2012: Stefano Pilati and the rebuilding of the commercial house

After Tom Ford's departure in 2004 Stefano Pilati became creative director. Kering later described his period as the rebuilding and repositioning of a French luxury house. Pilati worked with volume, a strict suit, complex femininity and accessories, seeking to join Saint Laurent's archival intelligence to the market of the 2000s. [19]

His work shows the difficulty of a heritage brand. Too literal a quotation looks like a museum reproduction; too distant a direction raises the question of why the name is kept. Pilati chose an evolutionary model: recognisable jackets, blouses, colour and silhouette discipline existed without the constant reproduction of a few famous objects.

In 2008 PPR completed the sale of YSL Beauté to L'Oréal for €1.15 billion and retained the rights to the fashion brand. From this point beauty products continued to use the name and monogram under a licensing model, but their operational history separated from the fashion house. [20]

17. 2012–2016: Hedi Slimane, the name Saint Laurent and the total direction of the image

In March 2012 Hedi Slimane was appointed creative director with responsibility for all collections and the overall image of the brand. He returned to the house where he had led the men's line until 2000. [21]

Slimane changed the ready-to-wear marking to Saint Laurent, citing the name and graphics of the 1966 Rive Gauche line. The full corporate name and the YSL monogram did not disappear, but the centre of communication shifted to a strict horizontal wordmark. The renaming sparked debate, since it looked like a removal of the founder's name, although historically it referred to his own ready-to-wear system. [1]

The Slimane period was characterised by a narrow rock silhouette, black leather, youth music, authorial photography and total control of the image. He turned the campaign, the casting, the store, the packaging and the architecture into a single visual regime. Commercial growth showed the strength of such centralisation, but critics debated whether the rock stylisation replaced the complexity of historical Saint Laurent.

In 2015 the creative operations moved to 24 rue de l'Université. The house also made couture pieces for private and stage projects, but this did not mean a restoration of the public haute couture calendar of 1962–2002 or a return to the former institutional model. [1]

18. Since 2016: Anthony Vaccarello and the discipline of a recognisable silhouette

In April 2016 Anthony Vaccarello was appointed creative director. He kept the short name Saint Laurent and built his period around a long line, a strong shoulder, the open body, leather, transparency, a high heel, black and a large-scale evening staging. [22]

Vaccarello does not try to reconstruct the archive collection by collection. He draws from it the stable tensions: the male jacket against bare skin; discipline against danger; Parisian strictness against theatre; a black ground against gold or saturated colour. Thus the legacy works as a grammar rather than a catalogue of quotations.

In 2017 the operational divisions moved to the headquarters at 37 rue de Bellechasse. The period's visual culture actively uses the architecture of Paris and desert landscapes, monumental sets and cinematic light. The show becomes a temporary space of the brand that spreads through photography and video far beyond the physical audience.

The length of the Vaccarello period matters in itself: by 2026 he has led the house for ten years. This has allowed not a series of relaunches but a stable contemporary identity in which the historical codes read even without a literal YSL monogram.

19. Rive Droite, Babylone and Saint Laurent Productions

In 2019 Saint Laurent opened Rive Droite — a cultural and commercial space whose name reverses the historical formula of Rive Gauche. The Paris venue took over the former premises of colette; after a renovation it reopened in February 2025. Here clothing sits beside books, music, object design, exhibitions and limited projects. [23]

In 2023 Saint Laurent Productions was created. The house describes the division as the first full film-production company inside a fashion house. Its filmography includes work by Pedro Almodóvar, Jean-Luc Godard, Jacques Audiard, David Cronenberg, Paolo Sorrentino, Jim Jarmusch and other directors. Saint Laurent's role varies by project, so the specific credit should be checked in the titles of each film. [24]

Cinema continues Saint Laurent's historical link with Catherine Deneuve and stage costume, but the contemporary model changes the level of involvement: the brand does not only dress a hero or fund an advertising short but enters the production of feature films.

In 2023 a large flagship opened on the Champs-Élysées, and in 2024 the book and cultural space Saint Laurent Babylone on rue de Grenelle. These initiatives turn retail into an infrastructure of cultural selection: the brand sells not only an object but its own system of taste. [1]

20. The archive, two museums and the separation of legacy and brand

The Fondation Pierre Bergé – Yves Saint Laurent was created after the close of haute couture to preserve the work of 1962–2002. The Musée Yves Saint Laurent Paris reports a collection of roughly 34,000 objects: clothing, accessories, sketches, photographs, documents and personal items. The foundation had earlier described the core as around 5,000 couture pieces and 15,000 accessories. [26]

In October 2017 museums opened in Paris and Marrakech. The Paris museum is housed in the historical building on avenue Marceau; the Marrakech complex, designed by Studio KO, includes a permanent and a temporary exhibition, an auditorium and a research library. [28]

These institutions preserve the legacy of Yves Saint Laurent but are not an archive department of Kering. The commercial house may turn to the history, but the museum mission includes conservation, scholarly documentation and public access. The separation matters for credibility: a brand heritage page and a museum catalogue answer different questions.

The archive also creates a problem of canon. The more often the Smoking, Mondrian and safari are published, the more strongly they displace the less-known collections, the work of employees, everyday clothing and ambiguous objects. VANSMITHLAB's task is to use the icons as an entry but not as the full history.

21. Saint Laurent in 2026: management, economics and categories

Since 2 January 2025 the CEO of Saint Laurent has been Cédric Charbit. Anthony Vaccarello remains creative director. The house belongs to Kering and operates in the categories of women's and men's ready-to-wear, bags, small leather goods, footwear, jewellery, eyewear and accessories. Beauty and fragrances are managed by L'Oréal under a separate model. [25][27]

According to Kering, Yves Saint Laurent's revenue for 2025 was €2.6 billion, down 8% in reported terms and 6% on a comparable basis. Recurring operating income was €529 million, and the operating margin was maintained at 20%. These figures describe a single reporting period and are not an assessment of artistic quality. [27]

The contemporary house combines its own retail and selective wholesale, international production, an accessories business and high-cost cultural communication. Its stability depends not only on the runway but on the quality of the leather goods, footwear, client service, stores, logistics and the ability to preserve the value of a recognisable code between seasons.

The current scale makes Saint Laurent at once a cultural author and a large commercial organisation. An encyclopaedic assessment must therefore hold the two levels side by side: the collection as a statement and the company as a system of labour, raw material, finance, rights and global distribution.

22. Critical questions: freedom, the body, cultural references and sustainability

The history of Saint Laurent is often told in the language of liberation: trousers, the tuxedo and the transparent blouse gave the woman new roles. This formula has a basis but must not turn into a universal truth. Clothing offers a possibility, not a guarantee of freedom; the commercial image of a slender, young and sexualised body also creates new norms of exclusion.

Saint Laurent's cultural collections enlarged the European fashion vocabulary and sustained craft techniques, but often turned vast regions and societies into aesthetic themes: "Africa", "Russia", "China", "India". A contemporary publication must name specific sources and acknowledge the colonial context, without erasing the historical complexity behind the general word "inspiration".

Contemporary Saint Laurent publishes commitments on traceability, certified materials, circularity and staff training through the Saint Laurent Couture Institute. The company reports the reworking of leather remnants and the use of metal-free tanning in certain products. These statements are corporate data and require verification of scale, methodology and the share of total volume. [35]

A luxury house does not become sustainable only because an object is expensive and durable. Impact includes animal materials, cotton, wool, chemical processes, energy, international logistics, packaging, stores and the constant production of novelty. A correct analysis assesses specific indicators, not the brand's aesthetic restraint.

The central question of legacy remains open: can a house founded on a personal name preserve its critical energy after turning into a corporation? Saint Laurent shows that it can — but only if the new leadership uses the archive as an instrument of choice rather than a set of guaranteed-selling symbols.

23. Conclusion: the house as a technology of constant return

Saint Laurent changed fashion through repeatable transformations. The pea coat became couture; the tuxedo a women's evening alternative; ready-to-wear an autonomous authorial project; painting a construction of a dress; cinema a production activity of a fashion house. In all cases a ready cultural form passed into a different system and changed its meaning.

Yves Saint Laurent's legacy does not consist of endlessly reproducing the year 1966. His method lay in a precise observation of what already exists outside couture: the men's suit, the street, music, uniform, painting, cinema, vintage and the personal style of the woman. The contemporary Saint Laurent therefore remains convincing when it continues this way of looking, not only when it quotes the result.
