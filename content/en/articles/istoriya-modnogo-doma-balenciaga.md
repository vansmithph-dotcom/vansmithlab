Publication passport

Working title

The history of the Balenciaga fashion house: from Getaria, gazar and architectural couture to Nicolas Ghesquière, Demna and Pierpaolo Piccioli

Editorial thesis

Balenciaga's history consists of several ruptures rather than the continuous evolution of a single company. Cristóbal Balenciaga created, in Spain and Paris, a couture system founded on knowledge of craft, the properties of fabric, the freedom of the body and precise control of volume. In 1968 he closed his houses, refusing to adapt his own model to ready-to-wear. The brand was relaunched in the 1980s as ready-to-wear and became influential again under Nicolas Ghesquière, then, under Demna, turned into a laboratory of contemporary luxury where couture coexisted with hoodies, sneakers, internet memes and institutional critique. The era of Pierpaolo Piccioli returns the question to its starting point: is Balenciaga a set of archival forms or a method of creating space around the person?

Reader question

How did Cristóbal Balenciaga change the construction of clothing, why did his house close in 1968, how was the label restored, and what today connects historical couture with contemporary Balenciaga?

Editorial promise

To show Balenciaga at once as the biography of a master, the history of a couture institution, an object of corporate relaunch and a contemporary design system. The material separates the Spanish companies Balenciaga and Eisa from the Paris house, and the founder's historical legacy from the decisions of subsequent creative directors.

Material boundaries

The primary object is the history of Balenciaga from Cristóbal Balenciaga's first registered business in San Sebastián in 1917 to the state of the house within Kering as of 5 August 2026.

The Spanish and French flags in the title denote the founder's origin and the institutional history of the Paris house, not a dual contemporary jurisdiction.

C. Balenciaga, Balenciaga y Cía, Cristóbal Balenciaga, Eisa Costura and the Paris Maison Balenciaga are not merged into a single legally continuous company.

Formulations such as "invented modern fashion", "created the sack dress first" or "the only true couturier" are treated as historical assessments and quotations, not as neutral measurable facts.

The Michel Goma and Josephus Thimister periods are included as part of the relaunch, although their archive is less well documented than the Ghesquière, Wang and Demna eras.

The advertising-campaign scandal of 2022 is described precisely, without reproducing the contested images and without unverified accusations against individual employees.

Balenciaga's financial data is not broken out by Kering as standalone revenue; segment figures cannot be presented as the sales of a single house.

Pierpaolo Piccioli's first full system is still forming; the assessment of his era at the cut-off date is preliminary.

Short answer

Cristóbal Balenciaga opened his own couture business in San Sebastián in 1917 and his Paris house on Avenue George V in 1937. He changed the female silhouette through volumes, displaced waistlines, a free back, the sack dress, the baby-doll and sculptural constructions in gazar. In 1968 the master closed his houses and left fashion. The label was relaunched in 1986, gained new influence under Nicolas Ghesquière, entered Kering in 2001 and became a global phenomenon under Demna, who brought couture back in 2021. Since July 2025 creative direction has been led by Pierpaolo Piccioli, and the CEO is Gianfranco Gianangeli. In the first half of 2026 Kering reported that Balenciaga was passing through a difficult creative transition, while leather goods remained a strong category.

Main article

1. Getaria: the port, the mother's craft and an early eye for fabric

Cristóbal Balenciaga Eizaguirre was born on 21 January 1895 in Getaria, a small fishing town on the Basque coast. His father was a fisherman and died when the boy was eleven. His mother, Martina Eizaguirre, worked as a seamstress and served, among others, the family of the Marquises of Casa-Torres. Balenciaga's early biography therefore joins two worlds: the practical craft of a working household and the observation of an aristocratic client's clothing.

This environment explains one of the enduring traits of his method. Balenciaga did not perceive clothing as a drawing that must then be technically executed by someone else. For him, material, fit, seam, balance and movement formed a single process. The later architectural quality of his couture grew out of direct work with the hands, not out of a wish to turn a dress into a static sculpture.

2. San Sebastián: training in a city of European fashion

By 1907 Balenciaga was registered in San Sebastián and had begun to learn tailoring in enterprises connected to Paris fashion. In the early twentieth century the city was a summer residence of the court and an international resort. Here Spanish aristocracy, French suppliers, a tourist economy and a seasonal culture of bespoke ordering all met.

Stories of how the Marquise of Casa-Torres gave the adolescent a dress to copy and then helped his training recur in many biographies, but the details differ. For an encyclopaedic article it is safer to record what is confirmed: the marquise was an early patron and client, and San Sebastián gave Balenciaga a professional environment in which local sewing practice was directly connected to Paris.

3. 1917–1937: C. Balenciaga, Eisa and the Spanish system of houses

In 1917 the twenty-two-year-old Balenciaga registered the business C. Balenciaga on Calle Vergara in San Sebastián. The enterprise soon became Balenciaga y Cía with the participation of the Lizaso sisters. After the partnership ended in 1924 the designer again worked under his own name. According to the museum, that same year the company already employed 71 people — an indication that this was not a small individual workshop but a developed couture operation.

In 1927 a second label appeared, first Martina Robes et Manteaux, then Eisa Costura — the name linked to his mother's surname, Eizaguirre. Eisa expanded to Madrid in 1933 and Barcelona in 1935. This system allowed him to serve different markets while retaining authorial control. Later, garments from the Spanish workshops were often labelled Eisa, so museum objects require a precise determination of label, city and date.

4. The Civil War and the decision to move to Paris

The Spanish Civil War destroyed the familiar client and economic environment. Balenciaga closed or suspended part of his Spanish operations and moved the centre of his activity to Paris. The move was not a rejection of Spain: visual, religious and constructive sources continued to define his work. What changed was his institutional position — a master from a Spanish resort entered the system of Paris haute couture.

Paris offered access to fabrics, embroidery houses, suppliers, the international press and a global clientele. At the same time it demanded participation in the seasonal calendar and constant comparison with Chanel, Schiaparelli, Mainbocher, Dior and other houses. Balenciaga managed to use this system while keeping a rare distance from publicity.

5. 1937: the house at 10 Avenue George V

In 1937 Balenciaga opened a couture house at 10 Avenue George V and presented his first Paris collection. The address became an important part of the label's identity: here were the salons, fitting rooms and workshops; later the house's first fragrance was named Le Dix. In 2021 the contemporary company returned couture to precisely this historical location.

The first collections drew attention with their technical precision and Spanish historical references. As early as 1939 the Infanta line linked the silhouette to portraits of the Spanish court. Yet the influence of painting did not mean copying costume: Balenciaga translated historical proportion into contemporary construction, cleaned away decoration and rebuilt the relationships between shoulder, waist and hip.

6. Spain as a visual archive: Velázquez, Zurbarán, the matador and religious dress

Balenciaga's work regularly featured black lace, stiff white collars, the bolero, forms of the matador's costume, the volumes of the infantas and the restrained monumentality of religious dress. These references connect him to Velázquez, Goya, Zurbarán and the material culture of Catholic Spain. But the exact source of a specific garment requires museum or archival attribution.

The main result of the Spanish influence was not decorative exoticism but a sense of contrast. Deep black worked beside pink, red or ivory; a severe surface concealed complex construction; a closed front could combine with a dramatic back. This tension between austerity and spectacle later became one of the ways to recognise Balenciaga without a logo.

7. Balenciaga's method: fabric before the drawing

Contemporaries stressed that Balenciaga could himself construct a pattern, cut, assemble and fit a garment. Unlike a designer who hands a sketch to the workshop, he understood every stage of production. The phrase "the fabric decides" precisely conveys the principle: the material did not fill a form conceived in advance but determined the construction that was possible.

This approach required prolonged repetition. Many innovations did not arise in a single striking gesture but developed over several seasons: the back became freer, the waist gradually disappeared, the sleeve merged with the body, and volume moved away from the figure. The history of Balenciaga's silhouettes is therefore better read as a consistent investigation than as a list of sudden inventions.

8. The space between body and clothing

Balenciaga's key contribution was the creation of a controlled space between body and fabric. Clothing could fit at the front and stand away from the back, surround the shoulders in a cocoon, widen at the hips or hover around the figure. The body remained the centre of the construction but ceased to be the only contour the dress was obliged to repeat.

Freedom of volume does not equal shapelessness. For fabric to stand away from the body while keeping its balance, a precise grain line, distribution of weight, internal support and an understanding of movement were required. Balenciaga's sculptural quality functioned only because it was engineered in relation to the human being.

9. 1947–1953: tonneau, cocoon and a new construction of the back

In 1947 the tonneau, or barrel line, appeared: a rounded body that altered the familiar geometry of waist and hips. In the early 1950s the cocoon and semi-fitted lines continued the movement away from the rigid hourglass. The front of a garment could maintain discipline, while the back gained volume and air.

This method is especially visible in coats and jackets. Instead of decorative complication, Balenciaga worked with large planes, one-piece sleeves, displaced seams and a smooth transition of shoulder into back. The garment seemed simple only after the complexity had been hidden inside.

10. 1955–1958: tunic, sack and baby-doll

The tunic dress of 1955 loosened the dress's dependence on the natural waist. The sack dress of 1957 made this rupture explicit: a straight silhouette let the fabric fall around the body without the traditional emphasis on bust, waist and hips. The press reaction was contradictory, but the form quickly entered mass fashion and became one of the precursors of the 1960s silhouettes.

The baby-doll of 1958 continued the abstraction through a short trapezoidal volume. It is important not to describe these forms only as the liberation of women: they arose within a system of expensive individual couture and offered a new aesthetic, but did not abolish the social constraints of fashion. Their historical radicalism lies in shifting the visual centre of the figure.

11. Gazar and the alliance of designer and fabric maker

In the late 1950s Balenciaga worked with Gustav Zumsteg and the Swiss house Abraham on gazar — a light yet stiff silk material capable of holding volume. The fabric became the technical condition of the late constructions. It allowed large forms to be created without a heavy frame and translated the dress into the realm of almost geometric abstraction.

The history of gazar shows that innovation in fashion rarely belongs to one person. The designer formulates the task, the manufacturer develops the material, the workshop finds the treatment, and the client tests the garment through movement. A correct article therefore names the collaboration rather than turning the fabric into a magical individual invention.

12. The client, the fitting and the hidden engineering of couture

Balenciaga dressed Mona von Bismarck, Gloria Guinness, Ava Gardner, Pauline de Rothschild and other influential clients. His couture was built on individual fittings and the ability to adapt an experimental form to a specific body. Even radical volumes had to allow the wearer to sit, move and enter a room.

In photographs the late dresses look almost seamless, yet the V&A reveals the complexity of their construction: whole panels, the absence of side seams, hidden fastenings and the precise joining of parts. The minimalism of the surface was the result of a great deal of handwork, not of its absence.

13. The 1960s: minimalism, abstraction and the envelope dress

In the 1960s Balenciaga's language became increasingly reduced. Colour and form worked as large autonomous fields; decorative detail gave way to proportion, curve and emptiness. Dresses could resemble liturgical vestments, Japanese constructions or abstract sculpture while retaining a couture function.

The envelope dress of 1967 pushed the experiment to the border of wearability. The four-pointed form in gazar was admiringly received by the press but practically difficult for the client. This object matters precisely as an investigation of fabric's possibilities rather than as a commercial hit. It shows that Balenciaga's couture served the client and tested the limits of clothing at the same time.

14. 1968: the closing of the houses as a deliberate decision

In 1968 Cristóbal Balenciaga presented his last collection and closed his couture houses. The museum links the decision to the changing industry: haute couture was losing weight, ready-to-wear was becoming the main system of contemporary fashion, and the master did not wish to rebuild his own method to fit the new scale. The closure was not the bankruptcy of an aesthetic but a refusal to continue under conditions he did not accept.

Balenciaga died on 24 March 1972. Closing during the creator's lifetime sharply distinguishes his history from houses that continued seasonal production under successors. Between historical couture and the contemporary brand there is a real institutional rupture that must be visible in the site's chronology.

15. A legacy without an active house: pupils, museums and copying

Balenciaga's influence continued through André Courrèges, Emanuel Ungaro, Hubert de Givenchy and other designers who worked alongside him or developed related principles. The lesson was not a specific form but the discipline of the cut, attention to the back, purity of line and the ability to begin a project from the material.

Museums played an important role in turning Balenciaga into a canon of design history. The exhibition The World of Balenciaga at the Met in 1973, subsequent retrospectives and the museum in Getaria secured access to the objects and the technique. At the same time the market copied the sack, cocoon and baby-doll, often simplifying the internal construction into an external contour.

16. 1986–1997: the ready-to-wear relaunch

In 1986 the rights to Balenciaga were acquired by the Jacques Bogart group, which relaunched the label as ready-to-wear. This moment changes the type of object: a historical house founded on individual couture becomes a brand that must operate through serial collections, licences, perfume and international distribution.

Michel Goma led the early stage of the relaunch, and in 1992 creative direction passed to Josephus Thimister. His minimalist collections returned attention to the founder's structural drama, but the house's commercial position remained weak. The period matters as a laboratory: without it, the later revival under Nicolas Ghesquière would look sudden.

17. Nicolas Ghesquière: the archive as science fiction

Nicolas Ghesquière became creative director in 1997 and worked with the archive as a source of the future rather than of nostalgia. Spanish motifs combined with futurism, sporting materials, a robotic surface and the culture of the late twentieth century.

His collections returned critical influence to Balenciaga and showed that heritage can be continued through contradiction. The architecture of form was preserved, but the objects of research became trousers, mini-dresses, jackets, synthetic fabrics and the modern woman moving through the city. In 2012 the house and the designer announced the end of their collaboration after fifteen years.

18. The City bag and the shift from collection to global product

In 2001 the bag known as Le City or the Motorcycle bag appeared. Its soft construction, external straps, metal hardware and deliberately informal surface set it apart from the rigid status bags of traditional luxury. It became an important part of Balenciaga's transition from an editorially influential ready-to-wear label to a large-scale international business.

The history of the City bag shows the economic role of the accessory. A collection idea may attract the press, but a bag creates a repeatable product recognisable off the runway. At the same time, the claim that the model "saved the house" requires caution: Balenciaga's success was the result of a linkage of design, ownership, distribution and category expansion.

19. Kering and the scaling of an international house

In 2001 Balenciaga joined the Gucci Group, later part of Kering. The new structure provided capital, a retail network, operational functions and the ability to develop leather goods, footwear, menswear and stores. The house ceased to depend on a single collection reputation and began to build a global model.

Corporate support does not make creative work automatic. On the contrary, Balenciaga constantly balances between experiment and the need to create large commercial categories. This conflict is especially visible in the Ghesquière and Demna periods, when the most radical shows coexisted with bags, sneakers and logo products.

20. Alexander Wang: sporting clarity and a short transition

After Ghesquière's departure Alexander Wang became creative director. His period of 2012–2015 strengthened sportiness, black-and-white graphics, mesh materials and clear commercial silhouettes. He drew on the archival architecture but made it smoother and more accessible for global retail.

In retrospective history Wang often finds himself between two more strongly canonised eras. Yet he sustained the house's international scale and revealed the limit of a strategy in which a flawless product does not always create an equally distinguishable cultural position. The collaboration ended in 2015.

21. Demna: the everyday as the material of luxury

Demna was appointed artistic director in October 2015. He brought to Balenciaga his experience at Vetements and a view of clothing as a social document. The work jacket, the hoodie, the office suit, the shopping bag, the blanket, the travel bag and the delivery-service uniform became material for luxury design through a change of scale, cut, context and price.

His connection to Cristóbal appeared not in visual resemblance but in the work with volume and distance from the body. The oversize puffer, the coat with a shoulder set back or the enormous suit continued the investigation of the space around the figure. At the same time Demna added irony, digital culture, a post-Soviet experience and a critique of consumption — elements absent from the historical house.

22. Sneakers, the logo and the economy of a new recognisability

Triple S, Speed and other footwear models helped Balenciaga become one of the centres of luxury streetwear. The massive sole, the deliberately complex layering and the joining of a sporting form with a high price changed the sneaker market. Logo hoodies, T-shirts and caps made the label easily legible on social networks.

Commercial success created a risk: product recognisability could obscure the constructive content. The house itself tried to restore the top of the pyramid through couture, complex shows and rare pieces. For the article it is important not to oppose the "real Balenciaga" and streetwear: both systems were part of one strategy but performed different cultural and economic functions.

23. Digital fashion, game worlds and the show as a cultural format

Under Demna, Balenciaga experimented with show formats: digital films, game spaces, red carpets, artificially created weather and scenographies that became media images in their own right. The collection ceased to be only a set of garments and turned into a managed environment of perception.

Collaborations with popular culture and gaming platforms widened the audience but required a precise description of rights and partners. Such an approach matched the digital economy of attention: the house produced not only a product but images, memes, avatars and events that spread faster than physical clothing.

24. 2021: the return of haute couture

On 7 July 2021 Balenciaga presented its 50th couture collection — the first since 1967. The return took place in the historical space of Avenue George V and became a way to connect the contemporary label with the founder's workshop. In the collection, couture techniques were applied not only to evening dresses but to T-shirts, jeans, coats and everyday typologies.

This gesture did not restore the institution of 1937 literally. Contemporary couture existed inside the global Kering brand and performed several functions at once: it preserved craft, created unique client garments, strengthened the prestige of the mass categories and allowed Demna to prove that his streetwear language could withstand the highest level of execution.

25. 2022: the advertising crisis and the question of institutional responsibility

In November 2022 two advertising campaigns provoked international criticism. In one, children held plush bear-bags with elements interpreted as BDSM paraphernalia; in another, a fragment of a document connected to a US Supreme Court decision on legislation about child sexual abuse material appeared on the set. The house removed the images, and Demna and the CEO apologised.

The crisis showed that responsibility cannot be reduced to a photographer, a props stylist or a single approving figure. In a large company an image passes through a creative, production, legal, marketing and executive chain. After the scandal Kering announced the creation of group-wide brand-safety oversight. For the site this case matters as an example of governance, not as a pretext for repeating sensational social-media versions.

In March 2023 the first post-crisis show was deliberately focused on the construction of the clothing. Reputational damage cannot be measured only by online reactions: it affected sales, relations with celebrities and trust in the control process. At the same time the brand retained the possibility of recovery thanks to product, couture and long-standing cultural significance.

26. 2025–2026: Pierpaolo Piccioli and a return to method

In 2025 Demna moved to Gucci. On 19 May Kering announced Pierpaolo Piccioli as the new creative director of Balenciaga from 10 July. That same year Gianfranco Gianangeli became CEO. Piccioli's first collection, Summer 2026, was presented in October 2025 and framed as a recalibration rather than archival reverence.

Piccioli concentrated on humanity, colour, lightness and couture volume. He drew on the sack, the cocoon and the work with distance from the body, but created his own materials and a contemporary softness. In July 2026 his first couture collection became the house's 55th collection. At the cut-off date it is still too early to assess the era as a completed system.

The financial context remains difficult. In its first-half 2026 report Kering stated that Balenciaga had experienced a harder quarter amid the creative transition and the rebalancing of the business, although leather goods remained a strong category. This underscores that the return to a couture code must work simultaneously as a cultural idea and a commercial strategy.

27. The codes of Balenciaga: what really belongs to the house

Balenciaga's enduring codes are not only the logo and the colour black. They include the volume of the back, the displaced waist, the cocoon, the sack, the one-piece sleeve, sculptural gazar, the contrast of a strict front and a dramatic rear, Spanish black, saturated colour, religious monumentality, technical purity and comfort inside a complex form.

The contemporary house added its own codes: the City bag, Ghesquière's sporting materials, extreme oversize, corporate typography, distressed surfaces, massive sneakers and the reimagining of everyday objects under Demna. They do not belong to Cristóbal, but they already belong to the history of Balenciaga. An encyclopaedia should date each code and not pass off a late language as an eternal heritage.

28. The business model, categories and current limits to growth

Today Balenciaga operates through ready-to-wear, leather goods, shoes, accessories, couture and fragrance. Leather goods and footwear create scale, ready-to-wear sustains creative identity, and couture forms the summit of symbolic value. In 2025 the house brought back perfumery through a collection of ten fragrances referencing Le Dix.

Kering does not publish standalone Balenciaga revenue in its regular segment reporting. The house belongs to Fashion & Leather Goods together with other labels, so segment figures cannot be used as Balenciaga's sales. Available corporate commentary makes it possible to assess the dynamics of categories and regions, but not to build a precise financial model of the brand without additional disclosures.

29. Critical questions: scarcity, provocation, sustainability and cultural memory

Balenciaga constantly uses the tension between a high price and an everyday exterior. The shopping bag, the worn shoe or the work jacket become luxury not only through construction but through the brand, restricted access and cultural capital. The critique of "expensive poverty" does not exhaust the phenomenon, but it reveals the ethical risk of turning social codes into a consumer game.

Provocation can be an analytical instrument only within a working system of responsibility. The 2022 crisis showed the limit of deliberate ambiguity: if an image touches vulnerable groups, the explanation of artistic risk does not replace control. The contemporary house is obliged to combine creative freedom with legal, ethical and production review.

Claims of sustainability require measurable data on materials, production volume, repair, logistics and the supply chain. The long life of an archival form or the high price of an item does not prove ecological sustainability. Likewise, preserving Cristóbal's name does not guarantee the preservation of his craft method — that is confirmed only by specific products and practices.

30. Conclusion: heritage as a way of working, not an archival form

Balenciaga's history matters because it shows several different ways of continuing a legacy. Cristóbal himself developed form through fabric and repetition; Ghesquière translated the archive into futurism; Demna applied the principle of volume to mass everyday life and the digital image; Piccioli returns attention to the human hand, colour and the couture method.

The house stays alive when heritage acts as a question rather than a ready answer: what can fabric do, how much space does the body need, where does the boundary between simplicity and engineering lie, how does couture speak to the present? Balenciaga loses its meaning not when it changes its aesthetic but when it stops asking these questions.
