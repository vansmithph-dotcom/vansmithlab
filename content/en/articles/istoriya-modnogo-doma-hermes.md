Working title

The history of the Hermès fashion house: from a Parisian harness workshop to Birkin, the carré, sixteen métiers and a global model of craft luxury

Editorial thesis

Hermès's longevity cannot be explained by a single scarce bag or the mastery of the hand stitch alone. The house consistently translated the original logic of the saddler's trade — the precise correspondence to function, respect for material, repairability and the craftsman's personal responsibility — into new categories: travel goods, clothing, silk, watches, jewellery, the interior, perfumery and beauty. Its history shows how craft becomes not the opposite of modernity but an infrastructure for managed renewal.

Reader question

How did a workshop for horse harnesses opened by Thierry Hermès in Paris in 1837 become an independent family luxury house whose bags, silk scarves, clothing and objects for the home are perceived at once as functional objects, signs of status and cultural artefacts?

Editorial promise

To trace Hermès as a history of mobility, materials, production organisation, artistic direction and control of distribution. The material separates confirmed events from corporate legend, explains the role of key objects without the language of advertising, and links the house's visual codes to its craft and business model.

Material boundaries

The primary object is the house and brand Hermès: harness making, leather goods, silk, ready-to-wear, watches, jewellery, perfumery, beauty, the home and the related spaces and communication.

John Lobb, Puiforcat and Saint-Louis are considered only in connection with the expansion of Hermès's craft portfolio; their independent history requires separate objects.

The chronological scope runs from 1837 to the house's position as of 5 August 2026.

The official chronology of Hermès is used as the primary institutional source. Where it shapes a legend of a product's origin, the wording retains its attribution and does not replace independent archival verification.

The names Kelly, Birkin and Bolide are analysed as historical objects and systems of construction, not as recommendations for purchase or investment assets.

Financial figures are given with date and period; market capitalisation and secondary-market prices are not used as a measure of cultural significance.

Disputes over access to Birkin, the use of exotic leathers and environmental claims are set out with an indication of the source, the status of the allegations and the company's response; legal allegations are not presented as proven facts.

Images are not embedded without an established origin, rights holder, permitted mode of use, caption, credit and alt text.

Short answer

Hermès was founded in Paris in 1837 as a harness workshop. The house grew by following changes in transport and everyday life: from the saddle and the travel bag to the zipper, the silk carré, clothing, watches, perfumery and objects for the home. Its influence rests on a rare combination of family control, integrated production, long training in a craft, the artistic freedom of individual métiers and largely owned retail. Birkin and Kelly became global symbols, but they are the result of a broader system in which an object is designed for use, age, repair and being passed on. [1][2][3]

The history of the Hermès fashion house

Hermès is often considered through two products — Kelly and Birkin. Such a view is convenient, but it distorts the scale of the history. These bags appeared not as autonomous icons but inside a house that for decades had been learning to link the object to the body, movement, load and time. To understand Hermès one must begin not with the celebrity but with the harness strap, not with the queue at the boutique but with the workshop, and not with the logo but with construction.

1. Before 1837: Paris, the carriage and the technical culture of movement

In the first half of the nineteenth century the horse was part of the urban infrastructure. Carriages moved people, goods and the post; the quality of harness affected not only appearance but safety, handling and the condition of the animal. The saddler worked on the boundary of several disciplines: he understood the properties of leather and metal, the anatomy of the horse, the distribution of load, the movement of the carriage and the social code of the client.

The harness was visible engineering. It had to withstand tension, not chafe the animal, allow the movement to be controlled and match the scale of the carriage. A client's wealth might show in the trim, but no amount of decoration could hide poor construction. It was precisely this joining of practical necessity and visual precision that became the founding culture of the future Hermès.

The house's later formula — "the aesthetics of function" — is not a retrospective embellishment of history. In the saddler's trade form genuinely grows out of use. The saddle stitch, the edges of the leather, the buckles, loops and straps read at once as technical solutions and as graphic language. When the horse ceased to be the main form of urban transport, this language did not disappear: it was carried over into luggage, bags, footwear, clothing and jewellery. [1][21]

2. 1837: Thierry Hermès's workshop on rue Basse-du-Rempart

The official history of the house begins in 1837, when Thierry Hermès opened a horse-harness workshop in Paris on the rue Basse-du-Rempart. Hermès describes the early objects as light, restrained and durable — qualities that answered the changing urban mobility. In 1867 the workshop's work received an award at the Exposition Universelle in Paris. [1]

The date of foundation matters not only as the beginning of a family line. Hermès arose before the modern fashion industry, before seasonal ready-to-wear and mass advertising. The primary product was an individually made tool, designed for long use. The notions of "collection", "fashion image" and "brand" therefore entered the house's history significantly later than its material discipline.

Six subsequent generations of the family developed the company in different economic conditions, but the early model left several enduring principles: the closeness of design and making; the high cost of an error; the tying of form to a specific use; the possibility of servicing an object; and a reputation based on repeatable quality rather than on a single public success.

3. 1867–1880: the recognition of craft and the move to 24 Faubourg Saint-Honoré

After Thierry Hermès his son Charles-Émile continued the business. In 1880 the workshops were moved to 24 rue du Faubourg Saint-Honoré, where a store opened. Saddles and harness were made to order at this address; it became not merely a point of sale but a space of encounter between client, craftsman, archive and the house's future categories. [1]

Faubourg Saint-Honoré linked craft to the geography of Parisian power, commerce and taste. But the meaning of the address for Hermès differs from that of an ordinary flagship. For a long time production took place directly above the store. The buyer saw the result, and the organisation kept a physical closeness between order and making.

In the twenty-first century 24 Faubourg functions as a symbolic centre: the store, an archival image, the name of fragrances and drawings, the place of annual windows and the source of architectural motifs. In an editorial piece it is important not to turn the address into a mystical formula. Its strength is explicable: a rare luxury house preserved one and the same urban anchor across several technological eras. [1][24]

4. From horse to automobile: Émile Hermès and the change in the way of life

The chief threat to a harness house at the start of the twentieth century was at the same time an opportunity. The automobile gradually changed transport, travel and luggage. The company could defend the old market or apply its knowledge of leather, fastening and load to new objects. Under Émile Hermès the house chose the second path.

In the official chronology the interwar period is described as a transition from harness and saddle goods to leather goods. What matters is not the fact of diversification itself but its logic: Hermès did not begin to produce just any fashionable thing. It followed the movement of the client. First the objects served the carriage; then the automobile, the train, the ship, sport, the city and the changed wardrobe. [1]

Émile Hermès also formed a collection of books, works, tools, fabrics and historical objects. Today the Collection Émile Hermès remains an internal source of drawings, forms and historical links. Here the archive works not as a museum of a completed past but as a research base from which creators extract a motif, a construction or a name and translate it into a new object. [1][25]

5. 1922–1928: the zipper, Bolide and the first new métiers

During a trip to Canada Émile Hermès noticed the American "close-all" system used on automobile equipment. In 1922 he obtained exclusive rights to its use; the system known today as the zipper began to be used in the house's luggage. [1]

In 1923 the Bolide was created — a travel object with rounded corners and a zipper, designed for car travel and placement in a boot. Official Hermès materials call it the first use of a zipper on a bag of the house; broader claims of the "first bag with a zipper in history" should be avoided without separate patent research. [5]

In the following years Hermès opened new directions: a men's golf jacket in 1925, jewellery in 1927, watches and sandals in 1928. These dates show that the house was turning into a system of objects for a way of life long before the postwar luxury conglomerate. Expansion moved toward categories for which a material or functional competence already existed: leather, metal, the strap, movement, fastening, time.

6. 1937: the carré — the square of silk as a wearable archive of drawing

In 1937 Hermès issued the first silk carré — Jeu des omnibus et dames blanches. The square format allowed the house to carry its culture of line from leather and harness into printed image. A scarf can be unfolded as a composition, folded as a colour fragment, worn on the body or used as a sign of belonging. [1]

The carré changed the scale of the house's communication. A saddle object requires a close acquaintance with the material; the scarf turns the drawing into a moving surface visible from a distance. Equestrian motifs, straps, carriages, botany, architecture, geography and fantastic narratives could coexist within one wearable archive.

Technically, silk requires its own chain of skills: drawing, colour separation, printing, the finishing of the edge. Hermès continues to invite artists of different generations and cultures, but the right to an image does not cancel editorial responsibility. When publishing historical carrés one must indicate the author of the drawing, the date, the title, the technique, the source of the image and the mode of use. The museum records of the Met confirm the presence of Hermès scarves of the 1950s in the Costume Institute collection. [9]

7. Robert Dumas: Kelly, Chaîne d'ancre and the language of functional elegance

Robert Dumas, son-in-law of Émile Hermès, took over the company in 1951. Even before this he had taken part in creating the house's key objects: the first carré, the bag later named Kelly, and the Chaîne d'ancre bracelet inspired by anchor chains in Normandy. [1]

The bag known today as Kelly was developed in the 1930s. In 1956 a photograph of Grace Kelly with this model spread widely, after which Hermès named the bag after the Princess of Monaco. It is important to separate three events: the creation of the form, its public association with Grace Kelly and the later fixing of the name. [1][6]

Kelly shows how the house turns a constructive system into an enduring code. The trapezoidal body, the flap, the straps, the swivel clasp, the lock and the handle can shrink, grow and be carried into other categories while remaining recognisable. This is not simply a silhouette; it is a grammar of details that can be quoted without printing a large logo.

Chaîne d'ancre works in a similar way. The marine chain was turned into a jewellery module that preserves the sense of weight and joining. Hermès regularly builds design on a transition: a utilitarian detail becomes an ornament without losing the memory of function.

8. The orange box, the carriage sign and visual identity

Hermès's visual identity is not reducible to a wordmark. The motif of the carriage and the waiting groom refers to a painting by Alfred de Dreux from the Collection Émile Hermès and preserves the original scene of the service of movement. What matters in it is the absence of an owner: the carriage is ready, the groom is waiting, and the person for whom the object is made stands outside the image.

The orange box arose not as a pre-designed global system. In Hermès's official account, after the Second World War the previous shades of packaging became unavailable owing to restrictions on paper, and a supplier could offer orange cardboard. The accidental material gradually became a sign of the house; Hermès indicates that the colour became a signature from 1960. [22]

This story is useful as an example of how a constraint can form an identity. However, it should be attributed to the house: it is corporate oral and archival tradition, not an independently reconstructed standard of the packaging industry. The box also shows the duality of Hermès — a bright outward surface conceals an object whose value must be revealed through material and use.

The bolduc ribbon links packaging to the annual theme. Thus the house's communication is not wholly separate from the object: the theme passes through the windows, the events, the drawings and the packaging, creating a temporary layer over the permanent codes.

9. 1960–1978: windows, women's clothing and Le Monde d'Hermès

In the early 1960s Leïla Menchari joined Annie Beaumel in the work on the windows of 24 Faubourg. From 1978 to 2013 Menchari independently created fantastic scenes for the store that joined Hermès objects with theatre, nature, travel and craft. The window became not a place of dense product display but a short visual novella. [1]

In 1967 Catherine de Karolyi was given responsibility for the women's wardrobe and until 1980 designed clothing and accessories, including the H buckle. The first women's ready-to-wear did not abolish Hermès's object culture: clothing was built as an extension of the body's movement, of leather, the buckle and the pocket. [1]

In 1973 Die Welt von Hermès appeared in Germany; the French version Le Monde d'Hermès followed two years later. The publication operates between catalogue, magazine and cultural map. It creates a context around the objects, but it cannot be used as an independent source for assessing the house: it is a primary corporate media object, valuable for understanding self-description, visual culture and authorial collaborations.

10. Jean-Louis Dumas: international expansion and the culture of the object

Jean-Louis Dumas took over Hermès in 1978. The official history links to him the expansion of geography, the development of new métiers and a more active joining of heritage with contemporary culture. That same year the watch business was formalised as La Montre Hermès. The portfolio also gained John Lobb, Puiforcat and Saint-Louis, which preserved their own names and craft specialisations. [1]

Jean-Louis Dumas's approach differed from a strategy in which a single logo spreads quickly across licensed categories. Hermès developed internal directions and acquired houses based on a specific know-how. Such a model scales with greater difficulty but allows the cultural difference between a shoe maker, silversmithing, crystal, silk and leather to be preserved.

International expansion was accompanied by the architectural individuality of the stores. Maison Hermès in New York opened in 2000, the building in Ginza by Renzo Piano in 2001, the Maison in Seoul in 2006. Each address translated the house's general codes into the local urban fabric rather than reproducing an entirely identical interior. [1]

Under Jean-Louis Dumas the idea of Hermès as a "house of objects" rather than only a fashion house took shape. This distinction helps explain why a saddle, a cup, a watch and a dress can exist side by side: they are united not by category but by a relationship to form, material and use.

11. 1984: Birkin and the transformation of a utilitarian bag into a social symbol

The official history of Birkin begins with the meeting of Jean-Louis Dumas and Jane Birkin on a Paris–London flight in 1984. Birkin said she could not find a roomy bag for the life of a young mother; Dumas sketched a rectangular bag with a flap, a saddle stitch and space for everyday things. [4]

The Birkin's construction joins a travel bag and a formal object. It is roomy enough for use but fitted with a system of straps, a lock and metal hardware that slows access and turns opening into a ritual. In this tension between utility and ceremony lies a large part of its cultural effect.

Later Birkin became a symbol of status, scarcity and the secondary market, yet these meanings should not be retrospectively attributed to the 1984 design. The history of the product and the history of its social value are different processes. The V&A included Jane Birkin's first Birkin in the exhibition Bags: Inside Out alongside Kelly, examining the bag through function, status, identity and production. [8]

The popular claim of the bag as a guaranteed investment requires refusal. An auction price depends on material, size, condition, provenance, rarity and the market cycle. VANSMITHLAB treats Birkin as an object of design and cultural economy, not a financial instrument.

12. Women's ready-to-wear: from Catherine de Karolyi to Nadège Vanhée

Hermès's women's clothing did not develop under the sway of a single permanent artistic image. Catherine de Karolyi created the house's early wardrobe; later different designers translated its material codes into clothing in different ways. The best-documented turn came in 1997, when Jean-Louis Dumas invited Martin Margiela to lead women's ready-to-wear. [10]

Margiela's period, which lasted until 2003, was built on comfort, the mature body, quality materials and hidden construction. His Hermès did not literally repeat the deconstructive language of his own label. The exhibition Margiela, The Hermès Years showed that the common interest of the two systems lay in precision of cut, bodily freedom and a refusal of demonstrative fashion effect. [10]

Jean Paul Gaultier led the women's line from 2003 to 2010, strengthening imagery, equestrian references and stage energy. Christophe Lemaire succeeded him in 2010 and until 2014 developed a calmer, more functional wardrobe. In 2014 Hermès appointed Nadège Vanhée-Cybulski; her first collection was presented in 2015. [11][12]

In 2026 Nadège Vanhée remains artistic director of women's ready-to-wear. Her work shows a model characteristic of Hermès: a designer is given a long period to develop a language, but exists within a system of materials, workshops and the artistic coordination of Pierre-Alexis Dumas. [3]

The history of the women's line should not be retold as a contest of famous surnames. For Hermès the continuity of questions matters more: how clothing moves, where the closure sits, how leather joins fabric, how colour works without a logo, and how a wardrobe ages more slowly than a seasonal image.

13. Men's ready-to-wear: 37 years of Véronique Nichanian and the transition to Grace Wales Bonner

Véronique Nichanian joined Hermès at the invitation of Jean-Louis Dumas in 1988 and for almost four decades shaped the men's ready-to-wear and the wider men's universe. Her approach was built on the study of material, colour, technical detail and an unobtrusive freedom of movement. The last collection was presented on 24 January 2026. [13]

The rare length of this period matters in itself. In an industry where artistic direction often changes for short-term renewal, Nichanian could develop gradual differences of shoulder proportion, the weight of leather, the inner finish, a transformable detail, a shade and the interplay of the formal and sporting wardrobe.

On 21 October 2025 Hermès announced Grace Wales Bonner as the new creative director of men's ready-to-wear. Her first collection for the house is scheduled for January 2027. It is therefore correct, as of the article's preparation date, to speak of an appointment and a transitional period, but not to attribute to Hermès an aesthetic of a future collection not yet presented. [14]

The transition is especially significant because Wales Bonner develops her own practice at the intersection of clothing, craft, music and cultural history. But VANSMITHLAB must avoid predictions of exactly how she will change men's Hermès. Before the first collection this remains an open editorial question.

14. The sixteen métiers: how the house expands without a single chief designer

In 2026 Hermès describes its system as sixteen métiers: leather goods and the equestrian direction, women's and men's silk, women's and men's ready-to-wear, footwear, belts, hats, gloves, jewellery, watches, perfumery, beauty, furniture and art de vivre, tableware and petit h. [3]

The house has no single figure who personally designs all categories. Pierre-Alexis Dumas is responsible for overall artistic coordination, and the individual directions have their own creative or artistic directors. As of August 2026 these include Nadège Vanhée, Grace Wales Bonner, Pierre Hardy, Christine Nagel, Gregoris Pyrpylis, Cécile Pesce, Priscila Alexandre Spring and others. [3]

Such a structure allows different temporal rhythms to coexist. A perfume develops differently from a bag; a watch requires different engineering from a carré; furniture interacts with architecture, while petit h begins not from the market but from available residual material. The overall artistic directorate sets compatibility but does not erase specificity.

Here Hermès's organisational distinctiveness shows itself: heritage is held not only in an archive of images but in a distribution of competences. The house is stable as long as it knows who is responsible for form, who for material, who for making and who for the transmission of skill.

15. 24 Faubourg, Pantin and architecture as a production culture

In 1992 the leather workshops moved from the upper floors of 24 Faubourg to Pantin. The new complex gave the craftsmen more light and space; in 2013 it was extended by the Cité des métiers, which unites buildings and themed gardens. The RDAI project gained architectural recognition in France. [1]

The move shows that the preservation of craft does not mean the preservation of historical cramped space. Precise handwork requires ergonomics, light, training, logistics, humidity control and a safe organisation of materials. The modern workshop is not a romantic décor but a technological environment.

In 2025 Hermès opened its twenty-fourth leather site in France — the L'Isle-d'Espagnac manufacture, designed in prospect for 260 craftsmen. The official strategy at the end of 2025 indicates more than twenty leather workshops united around ten regional centres of skill. [1][2]

The architecture of the stores solves a different task: to create a "house for objects" in a specific city. Ginza by Renzo Piano, the Maison on Madison Avenue and other addresses are not copies of Faubourg. They preserve a recognisable rhythm of materials and light but include the local architectural logic. This matches the "multi-local" approach Hermès also uses in store assortment.

16. The store, the window, the magazine and the site as Hermès media

Hermès controls not only production but a large part of the context in which an object meets the buyer. More than 92% of revenue in 2025 came through a network predominantly made up of group-managed subsidiaries. The stores have buying freedom, so the assortment of different addresses is not fully identical. [2]

This model strengthens the role of the salesperson and the local store director. They do not merely execute a centralised plan but form a choice from a large number of references. The advantage is the possibility of adaptation and the preservation of a sense of discovery. The risk is the opacity of access to the most sought-after objects and the appearance of unofficial notions of necessary purchases.

The window, Le Monde d'Hermès and the annual theme create a narrative layer. The e-commerce site launched in the US in 2002 and in France in 2005; the global digital platform was updated in 2017. The online store does not fully replace the physical network but distributes categories and services across channels. [1]

For VANSMITHLAB this is an important example of a brand as publisher and curator. But corporate media must be read critically: they are reliable for the dates of their own initiatives, authors and stated strategy, but are not independent proof of cultural superiority or ecological efficiency.

17. Family independence, management and the conflict over the LVMH stake

Hermès remains a public company with a particular control structure. Official documents indicate that the group is controlled by the family group through Émile Hermès SAS and the family vehicles H51 and H2. As of 2026 the executive leadership is headed by Axel Dumas; Pierre-Alexis Dumas is responsible for the artistic direction. [15][16]

Family control does not automatically guarantee high quality, but it creates the conditions for a long investment horizon. The company can expand training and workshops more slowly than demand grows, without handing critical categories to mass licensees. The limit of this model is lower external transparency of internal decisions and a high dependence on the family's ability to transmit management.

In the 2010s independence became the subject of an open corporate conflict after LVMH accumulated a significant stake in Hermès. In 2014 the parties reached an agreement through the mediation of the president of the Paris Commercial Court: LVMH undertook to distribute Hermès shares to its shareholders, and the linked structures to refrain from new acquisitions for a set period. [17]

This episode matters not as a personal duel of two leaders but as a clash of luxury models. A conglomerate builds strength through a portfolio of houses and centralised resources; Hermès defended family control and its own pace. Both models can sustain craft, but they distribute capital, risk and power differently.

18. 2008–2021: Fondation, petit h, Apple Watch, Beauty and the school of crafts

In 2008 the Fondation d'entreprise Hermès was created, working with artistic creation, the transmission of skills, education, solidarity, biodiversity and the preservation of the environment. The foundation should be considered a separate institutional structure with its own programmes, not as proof of the social value of every commercial product. [1][18]

In 2010 Pascale Mussard launched petit h — a direction of "reverse creation" that begins from unused materials and parts from other métiers. The project does not solve the whole volume of production waste but institutionalises a different order of design: first the available material, then the possible object. [1][19]

In 2015 Apple and Hermès presented the Apple Watch Hermès. The partnership linked a digital device, an interface and a strap to the house's leather skill. It showed that a historic brand could take part in a quickly renewing technology category without trying to build the electronic platform itself. [1][20]

In 2020 Beauty became the sixteenth métier with Rouge Hermès. Here the house carried its work with colour, packaging material and interchangeable elements into cosmetics. In 2021 the École Hermès des Savoir-Faire opened, issuing a professional qualification in leather craft. The school is especially important to the growth model: a workshop can be built faster than a stable community of skilled workers can be formed. [1]

19. The codes of Hermès: the saddle stitch, leather, hardware, colour, humour and the annual theme

The saddle stitch is executed with two ends of a single thread passing toward each other through holes in the leather. Hermès stresses its strength: the damage of one stitch does not necessarily unravel the whole seam. In the house's visual language the stitching remains visible and speaks of construction rather than being hidden as a purely technical trace. [21]

Leather in Hermès is not a single material. Differences in grain, density, the tanning method, flexibility and patina change the behaviour of the same design. A model name without a material specified therefore describes only part of the object. For a research page about a bag a separate block is required: leather, lining, hardware, the method of assembly, the period and the condition.

Equestrian codes appear in belts, buckles, stirrups, bridles, chains, pockets and methods of joining. Their strength lies not in a literal depiction of a horse but in structural memory. Even an abstract metal link can be read as part of a harness.

The house's colour system is wider than the orange packaging. Carré, leather, enamel and clothing use complex combinations and unusual names for shades. Humour is also a constant code: trompe-l'œil, a disproportionate object, a hidden pocket or an unexpected function soften the solemnity of an expensive material.

The annual theme has acted since 1987 as a temporary research frame for the different métiers. It does not require all products to look the same; rather it creates a common question that can be translated into a drawing, a window, a name, an event or a packaging ribbon.

20. The business model and Hermès's position in 2026

Hermès's official strategy is formulated through three pillars: creation, craft and an exclusive, balanced distribution network. The company stresses independence, family ownership, vertical integration, owned retail and the production of a large share of objects in France. [2]

For 2025 Hermès reported revenue of €16.002 billion and recurring operating income of €6.569 billion, equivalent to 41.0% of revenue. For the first half of 2026 revenue was €8.163 billion; the headcount at the end of the period was 27,107. These figures describe the economic scale but do not replace an analysis of the quality of the objects or the conditions of production. [7]

At the end of 2025 the network counted 294 stores in 45 countries; more than 92% of revenue was generated through a network mainly managed by the group. Hermès stated that 75% of objects were produced in France and 55% in in-house and exclusive workshops. The wording must be accompanied by the year, since the production structure changes. [2]

Growth is limited not only by a marketing decision. Handwork requires training, workshops, materials, quality control and time. Yet scarcity is also part of the consumer experience and strengthens symbolic value. A correct analysis must hold both sides: the physical limit of capacity and the economic effect of limited availability.

In 2026 the house is at a transitional moment in men's fashion: Véronique Nichanian's period is over, Grace Wales Bonner has been appointed, but her first collection is expected in January 2027. The women's direction continues with Nadège Vanhée, and the overall artistic coordination is carried out by Pierre-Alexis Dumas. [3][13][14]

21. Critical questions: scarcity, access, the secondary market, animal materials and sustainability

The most visible dispute around Hermès concerns access to Birkin and Kelly. In 2024 an antitrust lawsuit was filed in the US alleging that buyers had to purchase other goods to gain the possibility of buying a Birkin. In September 2025 a federal judge again rejected the claims, noting that an alleged preference for the largest clients does not in itself constitute an antitrust violation. The allegations therefore cannot be presented as an established practice of the court. [26][27]

Even without a legal violation, the distribution system remains culturally significant. Non-public availability, a personal relationship with the store and a high secondary price create an asymmetry of information. For some buyers this is personal service and rarity; for others an opaque status filter. An encyclopaedic article should describe the mechanism and the positions of the sides rather than give advice on "obtaining a bag".

The secondary market prolongs the life of objects and demonstrates repairability, but at the same time turns the bag into a speculative asset. Rare auction records are not an average return. An editorial piece must not repeat the formula "better than gold" without representative data, commissions, the risks of forgery, storage and liquidity.

The use of leather, including exotic species, remains a fundamental ethical question. Hermès states that 98% of the hides used are a by-product of the food industry, and that supply is governed by an animal-welfare policy; by the end of 2023 all the company's crocodile skins came from certified sites. These figures are corporate indicators and require an independent assessment of the standards, the audit and the very admissibility of using animal materials. [23]

In sustainability the strong side of the model is durability, repair and control of production. Hermès reported more than 96,000 repairs in 2025 and a network of fifteen repair workshops. But a durable object is not automatically neutral: the extraction of material, tanning, energy, transport, packaging and the growth of volume also create impact. [19]

A correct conclusion therefore lies neither in unconditional admiration nor in the reduction of the house to an artificial scarcity. Hermès presents a rare case in which craft discipline is genuinely built into the business model; at the same time it is precisely the brand's economic strength that demands high transparency on questions of materials, access and environmental claims.

22. Conclusion: why Hermès stays contemporary

Hermès lived through the transition from the horse city to the automobile, from individual order to global retail, from the paper catalogue to e-commerce and from the mechanical watch to the connected device. The house did not preserve modernity by a constant rejection of the past. It used the past as a library of methods: the strap, the stitch, the buckle, the box, the drawing, the skill and the address could be linked anew to a new way of life.

Its key achievement is organisational. Hermès turned quality not into the promise of an advertising campaign but into a network of interdependent systems: artistic directors, craftsmen, schools, regional productions, the archive, repair, owned stores and family capital. The iconic object arises within this network and cannot be fully explained by a celebrity name or by scarcity.

Hermès's central contradiction also remains a source of its strength. The house speaks of useful, durable objects but sells them within a system of exclusive access and an extraordinarily high price. It defends handwork but operates as a global public company. It preserves equestrian memory but develops digital products. It is precisely the ability to hold these tensions — without resolving them once and for all — that makes Hermès not a historical relic but a working model of contemporary luxury.
