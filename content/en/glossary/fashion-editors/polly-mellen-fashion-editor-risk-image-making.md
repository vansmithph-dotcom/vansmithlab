# Polly Mellen

*Risk, Discipline and the Fashion Editor as Co-Author of the Image*

> Mellen turned the sittings editor from an invisible intermediary into an active co-author of fashion imagery, managing not only clothes but risk, pace, model, photographer and the event of the sitting itself.

## Editorial thesis

Polly Mellen matters not as Vogue’s legendary loud personality, but as evidence that the fashion editor authors relationships. Her work connected garment, body, photographer, location, production and editorial risk into one mechanism.

## Reader question

What exactly does a fashion editor author if the photographer presses the shutter, the designer makes the clothes and the model stands before the camera?

## Short answer

The editor creates the conditions in which separate professional decisions become one image: selecting, colliding, simplifying, defending risk and holding the production together until the frame says more than a set of clothes.

## 1. West Hartford, 1924: individuality before the industry

Polly Allen Mellen was born in West Hartford, Connecticut, on 18 June 1924. Vogue describes her years at Miss Porter’s School as an early refusal to look like everyone else: dress and gesture were already ways of declaring character. [S01][S02]

The detail matters for more than biography. In her mature work clothing was never a neutral commodity either; it had to intensify a person’s gesture, temperament and presence in the frame.

## 2. War and nursing work: fashion does not begin with gloss

After school during the Second World War, Mellen worked as a nurse’s aide in Virginia before moving to New York. [S01]

That beginning disrupts the convenient myth of an editor who moves directly into an elite fashion circle. Her professional discipline began before magazines, through work, hierarchy, responsibility and physical stamina.

## 3. Lord & Taylor and Saks: first the object, client and selling floor

In New York Mellen passed through retail at Lord & Taylor and Saks Fifth Avenue before moving into magazines. [S01]

Retail gave her practical knowledge of clothes as objects: how they fit, sell, behave next to a body and change the person wearing them. On set this later became an ability to see not a look in isolation but its potential inside an image.

## 4. Mademoiselle and Harper’s Bazaar: learning inside an editorial system

From Mademoiselle, Mellen reached Harper’s Bazaar through editor Sally Kirkland. Sources differ by one year: Vogue gives 1950, while Harper’s Bazaar gives 1951. VANSMITHLAB therefore records the beginning of the Bazaar period as 1950–1951 rather than forcing false precision. [S01][S19]

More important than the exact year is the mechanism: fashion editing was learned inside a team where the editor mediated among designer, garment, photographer and page.

## 5. Diana Vreeland: learning to search for the new rather than the safe

At Harper’s Bazaar Mellen came under Diana Vreeland’s influence. Vogue and Harper’s Bazaar both connect the early phase of her career to this school of editorial risk and curiosity. [S01][S19]

What Mellen inherited was less a visual formula than a principle: an editor must notice possibility before it becomes consensus. Later she would pay the same serious attention to a major couturier, an unknown designer and a strange idea that still had to prove itself in pictures.

## 6. Richard Avedon: friction becomes a thirty-year collaboration

Mellen recalled that Avedon initially did not want to work with her because he thought she was too loud. Vreeland insisted; on the first assignment Mellen barely spoke, and the tension grew into one of American fashion photography’s longest creative partnerships. [S01]

Partnership is the key word. The photographer authors the photographic language, but the fashion editor shapes casting, clothing, gesture, pace and risk. A powerful image emerges through negotiation, not through a service relationship.

## 7. Family and interruption: the career was not a straight line

After the short early Bazaar period, Mellen married, moved to Philadelphia and stepped away from intensive magazine work for a period; after divorce she returned to fashion. In 1965 she married Henry Wigglesworth Mellen. [S01]

The interruption matters because professional mythology often turns careers into uninterrupted ascent. Mellen’s actual path includes family, pause and return — followed by the period in which her influence became greatest.

## 8. Vogue in the mid-1960s: the sittings editor as a key author

Vogue connects Mellen’s move into American Vogue with Diana Vreeland and the mid-1960s. Sources place the transition around 1966–1967; the Avedon Foundation confirms a major Vogue publication on 15 October 1966. [S01][S11]

The term sittings editor describes her function better than stylist alone. She organised the situation of the image itself: clothes were only one layer beside model, photographer, location, hair, makeup, accessories, sequence and final edit.

## 9. The Great Fur Caravan, 1966: editorial ambition becomes an expedition

For the project remembered as The Great Fur Caravan, Mellen, Richard Avedon and Veruschka travelled to Japan. Vogue describes a five-week expedition, and the Avedon Foundation records the issue on 15 October 1966. [S01][S11]

The project matters for more than cost or exotic location. A fashion story becomes a long sequence in which clothes must survive climate, movement, cultural background and cinematic scale.

## 10. Location risk: production becomes part of meaning

The myth of Mellen often reduces risk to temperament. Large location stories show the practical side: risk requires logistics, backup plans, team discipline and the ability to keep clothing legible inside a complex environment. [S01][S02]

The editor therefore works as a producer of visual argument. The freer the image looks, the more invisible structure it often requires.

## 11. Twiggy, 1967: the sitting as precise choreography

A 1967 New Yorker report from Avedon’s studio shows Mellen at work with Twiggy: she helps adjust details of the look while the photographer builds pose, light and rhythm. [S13]

The document is valuable because it removes the romantic fog from the profession. Iconicity is made from minute corrections, a patient model, technical tempo and the shared attention of several people.

## 12. Penelope Tree: emphasising difference rather than correcting it

In Vogue’s The Editor’s Eye material, Mellen and Avedon emphasise Penelope Tree’s unusual elongated physicality with an intentionally too-small pantsuit. [S07]

The logic is opposite to normalisation. A fashion editor can identify a bodily characteristic that conventional styling might hide and turn it into the central expressive quality of the image.

## 13. The sitting as physical labour: clothes do not arrive in the frame by themselves

Vera Wang’s memories of working with Mellen stress the physical reality of the job: boxes, pins, garments, rain, constant movement and work on hands and knees. [S03][S15]

This is a useful corrective to the contemporary image of styling as choosing a few items from a rack. The editor is responsible for executing the decision in real time, so production discipline is equal to taste.

## 14. Only as good as the last sitting

Vera Wang recalled Mellen admitting that every new set of pictures could reopen the editor’s anxiety about whether the work was good enough. [S03]

That professional vulnerability explains her intensity better than the caricature of a formidable boss. Fashion imagery is a public result of collective work, which means the editor continually risks reputation on both personal and shared decisions.

## 15. Not one photographer: the editorial method has to change language

Although Avedon was central, Mellen worked with Irving Penn, Helmut Newton, Deborah Turbeville, Arthur Elgort, Patrick Demarchelier, Herb Ritts, Steven Klein and many others. [S04][S20]

If one fashion editor forces every photographer into the same pictures, collaboration becomes a template. Mellen’s strength was the ability to enter different photographic systems and intensify them rather than impose one recognisable styling effect.

## 16. The Bathhouse, May 1975: swimwear stops behaving like body advertising

In May 1975 Vogue published There’s More to a Bathing Suit Than Meets the Eye, photographed by Deborah Turbeville and fashion edited by Mellen. Its distant, decaying mood rejected the usual energy of beach fashion. [S05]

The story matters as collective authorship: Turbeville constructs the photographic world, while Mellen organises clothing within it. Later readings found traumatic associations in the images, so the work now requires critical context rather than unconditional celebration of provocation.

## 17. The Story of Ohhh…, the same May 1975 issue: another language of risk

The same issue carried The Story of Ohhh…, photographed by Helmut Newton and edited by Mellen. Vogue’s contemporary republication explicitly notes its sexual provocation and the way parts of its gender staging can read problematically today. [S06]

Its visual regime is entirely different from Turbeville’s: harder, more sexual and theatrical. Their coexistence shows Mellen’s range as an editor rather than one fixed aesthetic.

## 18. May 1975 as an editorial experiment: two conflicts in one issue

Condé Nast corporate photography director Ivan Shaw called the simultaneous publication of the Bathhouse and The Story of Ohhh… an extraordinary feat and linked it to Mellen’s courage. [S05][S06]

For VANSMITHLAB the larger point is that an editor does not have to keep one emotional temperature across an issue. Conflict between stories can make the magazine hold competing ideas about body, sexuality and fashion.

## 19. Mellen and Avedon: photography emerges between personalities

The Avedon Foundation and Vogue preserve decades of shared work, while The Met holds Avedon’s large 21 August 1975 portrait of Mellen herself. [S10][S20]

The portrait is symbolic: a person usually standing beside the camera becomes the subject. It reminds us that an “author photograph” often has a constant editorial interlocutor whose name the viewer notices less readily.

## 20. Nastassja Kinski and the snake, 1981: minimal clothing, maximal fashion image

Avedon’s photograph of Nastassja Kinski with a snake, made on 14 June 1981 and published by Vogue, became one of their best-known collaborations. Almost no clothing is present; a Patricia von Musulin bracelet, skin, pose and visual tension carry the fashion function. [S01][S12]

The image shows that fashion editing is not about quantity of garments. Sometimes editing means removing almost everything and leaving one object because it holds the composition and meaning.

## 21. From clothes to image: the editor manages relationships, not a wardrobe

The documentary In Vogue: The Editor’s Eye frames fashion editors as the people responsible for creating strong Vogue images rather than simply selecting clothes. Mellen appears alongside other major editors in the film. [S09]

That formulation captures her legacy: the work is a system of relations among garment, body, photographer, cultural moment and editorial risk.

## 22. Gender, sexuality and the critical reading of a legacy

Some of Mellen’s most famous stories are now inevitably read through changed understandings of consent, power, sexualisation and the male gaze. Vogue itself republishes The Story of Ohhh… with critical distance. [S06]

Historical importance does not end analysis. A strong profile can recognise Mellen’s professional courage while also asking whose risk became aesthetic and who carried the bodily cost of provocation.

## 23. The fashion show as a working instrument: emotion becomes editorial signal

Colleagues remember Mellen clapping, crying and physically expressing excitement at shows; designers monitored her reaction as a sign of editorial response. [S01][S02]

The theatricality was more than personality. It reflects an editor who must be emotionally engaged in fashion production and then translate that response into a decision about what deserves to be seen, supported or photographed.

## 24. Vera Wang: hard mentorship as transmission of the profession

Vera Wang remembered Mellen as both a demanding mentor and someone who understood the vulnerability of a young editor. Stories about a forgotten hat or the wrong clothes for a shoot function as lessons in responsibility for the whole production. [S03][S15]

Mellen transmitted not taste as secret knowledge but a professional standard: preparation, speed, physical readiness, attention to detail and the ability to defend an idea inside a team.

## 25. In front of the camera: the fashion editor becomes a visual character

Mellen herself appeared in advertising, on runways and in fashion documentaries; Vogue recalls Michael Kors and Gap campaigns, Unzipped and The Editor’s Eye. [S01][S02]

This did not replace her editorial role, but it produced a rare reversal: the person making other people’s images became a recognisable cultural character. Grey bob, disciplined clothes and expressive reactions formed a public sign of the profession.

## 26. Allure, 1992–1999: Vogue experience moves into a new beauty system

In 1992 Mellen became creative director of Allure and remained in the role until 1999; she retired fully in 2001. [S01][S04]

The move tested her method outside Vogue. Beauty imagery demands another proximity to skin, face and product, but the editorial principle remains: the picture has to be strong enough not to dissolve into its category.

## 27. From Vreeland to Mirabella and Wintour: surviving changes of era

Mellen’s career passed through radically different regimes at American Vogue. Vera Wang particularly stressed her ability not to freeze inside the aesthetics of one generation. [S03]

That is rare for a fashion editor: historical success can easily become a rule imposed on the future. Mellen could be a product of Vreeland and still work through the more minimal 1970s, celebrity culture of the 1980s and new hierarchies of the 1990s.

## 28. CFDA Lifetime Achievement, 1994: a hidden profession receives public recognition

In 1994 the Council of Fashion Designers of America honoured Mellen with a lifetime achievement award. Vogue records the moment while noting that she was still actively working. [S01]

The award matters because fashion’s public system usually celebrates designers and photographers more visibly than the people between them. In Mellen’s case the editorial role itself becomes worthy of named authorship.

## 29. 1995–2012: The New Yorker, Unzipped and The Editor’s Eye turn work into archive

In the mid-1990s Mellen continued beyond the conventional Vogue sitting, working with Avedon and Doon Arbus on In Memory of the Late Mr. & Mrs. Comfort for The New Yorker and appearing in Unzipped. In 2012 The Editor’s Eye made the fashion editor a direct subject of historical narration. [S01][S14][S09]

A profession long visible mainly through small credits became the subject of a film and book. That institutionalisation is part of Mellen’s legacy.

## 30. 1924–2024: the fashion editor as catalyst for the image

Polly Mellen died in December 2024 at the age of 100. For her centenary, colleagues described her as a co-creator able to pivot instantly and push an image beyond the safe solution. [S01][S02]

Her legacy is larger than “daring shoots.” The central lesson is that the fashion editor is a catalyst, connecting clothing, model, photographer, production and cultural moment until tension appears between them. That is what separates styling things from authoring a fashion image.
