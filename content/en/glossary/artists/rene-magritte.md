Publication passport

Working title

René Magritte: from Belgian Surrealism and The Treachery of Images to The Empire of Light, the repetition of motifs and a global visual brand

Editorial thesis

Magritte's significance rests not on a fantastic technique for the sake of fantasy but on a systematic investigation of the relations between the object, the word, the image and the title. His emphatically clear painting turns an everyday verisimilitude into an instrument of doubt.

Reader question

Why is a neatly painted pipe, apple, window, cloud or bowler-hatted man capable of unsettling a familiar reality more strongly than a free abstraction or the depiction of a dream?

Material boundaries

The primary object is René Magritte: his painting, graphic art, advertising, photography, films and public strategy.

Belgian Surrealism is treated as a collective intellectual milieu with the participation of Paul Nougé, Louis Scutenaire, Marcel Mariën and other authors.

The titles of the works were not always an individual decision of Magritte; the role of the friends and of the joint discussions is noted separately.

The history of the mother's suicide is not used as a universal psychological explanation of the covered faces and the fabric in the pictures.

The repetition of motifs is analysed at once as a philosophical method, a market practice and a means of producing recognisability.

Reproductions of the works remain the object of authors' and photographic rights; a rights review is mandatory before publication.

Short answer

René François Ghislain Magritte was born on 21 November 1898 in Lessines, Belgium. He studied at the Académie Royale des Beaux-Arts in Brussels, worked in applied graphics and advertising, and in the mid-1920s formed his own language of figurative Surrealism.

The Menaced Assassin, The Lovers, The False Mirror, The Treachery of Images, The Human Condition, Not to Be Reproduced, Time Transfixed, Golconda and the Empire of Light cycle show his enduring interest in the mismatch between the visible and the named.

1. Lessines, Châtelet and the early biography

René Magritte was born on 21 November 1898 in Lessines, in the Belgian province of Hainaut. The family moved repeatedly, and the artist's childhood passed in the industrial and provincial environment of Wallonia.

As an adolescent Magritte met Georgette Berger, whom he married in 1922. Their personal and working partnership lasted until the artist's death.

The early biography later became part of the public myth, yet Magritte himself resisted explaining art through a direct psychological determinism.

2. The mother's death and the limits of biographical interpretation

In 1912 Magritte's mother killed herself. This event is often used to explain the faces covered in cloth in his works.

The popular story that the artist saw his mother's body with the dress covering her face is not confirmed by documents and is reproduced as a biographical legend.

Fabric, the mask and the covered face in Magritte's works are more productively analysed within his general investigation of visibility, desire and the impossibility of complete knowledge.

3. The Academy in Brussels

In 1916 Magritte entered the Académie Royale des Beaux-Arts in Brussels.

The training did not become a complete artistic system for him, but it gave him the professional skills of drawing, composition and painting.

The early works included Cubist, Futurist and abstract experiments before the turn to a clear object-based manner.

4. Applied graphics, wallpaper and advertising

Before a stable recognition as a painter Magritte worked as a designer in a wallpaper factory, created posters, sheet-music covers, packaging and advertising graphics.

Later, together with his brother Paul, he undertook commercial commissions through the advertising practice Dongo.

The experience of applied graphics formed the precise outline, the flat space, the laconic object and the ability to create a memorable sign quickly.

5. The Belgian Surrealist group

Belgian Surrealism developed differently from André Breton's Paris group.

Paul Nougé, Camille Goemans, Louis Scutenaire, E. L. T. Mesens and other participants paid a special attention to language, the subversion of a familiar meaning and a collective intellectual experiment.

Magritte worked within this milieu, where the picture, the title, the magazine, the photograph and the short text were treated as interconnected forms of action.

6. Giorgio de Chirico and The Song of Love

The encounter with a reproduction of Giorgio de Chirico's painting The Song of Love became an important turn for Magritte.

He was struck by the possibility of joining ordinary objects such that their proximity ceased to be logical and became poetical.

Unlike automatism, Magritte sought a consciously constructed riddle, painted in the most clear way possible.

7. The Lost Jockey and the turn to a mature language

The Lost Jockey of 1926 is often called one of the first works of Magritte's mature Surrealist period.

A rider moves through a space where the trees are turned into chessboard-like or note-like forms, and the familiar landscape becomes a conventional stage.

The picture shows the artist's key method: not to invent a fantastic object but to disrupt the relations between familiar things.

8. Paris and the relations with French Surrealism

After the poorly received one-man exhibition of 1927, Magritte and Georgette moved to the vicinity of Paris.

The artist entered the circle of André Breton, Paul Éluard, Max Ernst, Joan Miró and other Surrealists.

Differences of outlook, personal conflicts and economic circumstances contributed to the couple's return to Brussels in 1930.

9. The Menaced Assassin

The Menaced Assassin of 1927 is constructed as a frozen frame of a crime film.

At the centre a man listens to a gramophone beside the body of a woman, while armed figures wait at the door and observers look in through the window.

The picture does not give a finished narrative: the viewer receives a multitude of signs but cannot reconstruct a single causal link.

10. The Lovers and the covered face

In the paintings The Lovers of 1928 the faces of a kissing pair are covered in cloth.

The gesture of closeness at once becomes a gesture of the impossibility of seeing and knowing the other.

The work is often linked to the artist's family tragedy, yet such a reduction closes off the wider question of desire, the image and the opacity of human closeness.

11. The False Mirror

In The False Mirror an enormous eye is filled with sky, clouds and the black disc of the pupil.

The viewer at once looks at the eye as an object and as if looks through it, as through a window.

The title, proposed by Paul Nougé, calls into question the idea of sight as a neutral mechanical reflection.

12. Words, images and The Treachery of Images

In a series of word-and-image paintings Magritte investigated the arbitrary link between the object, the image and the name.

The inscription "This is not a pipe" under a carefully painted pipe reminds the viewer: what is before him is not an object but an image of an object.

The work does not deny the obvious but separates the levels of reality that everyday language usually conflates.

13. The Human Condition

In The Human Condition a painting on an easel coincides exactly with the landscape outside the window.

The viewer cannot determine which part of the visible belongs to the outer world and which to the image on the canvas.

Magritte turns the classical metaphor of the picture-window into a problem: every sight is already organised by a frame and a cultural system of the image.

14. The window, the picture and the frame of sight

The window constantly appears in Magritte's painting as the boundary between the inner and the outer.

A broken pane can keep fragments of a depicted landscape, and a curtain can at once hide the space and point to its existence.

The frame is not a neutral edge: it determines what counts as an image, an object and reality.

15. Not to Be Reproduced and Edward James

Not to Be Reproduced of 1937 was created for the British poet and collector Edward James.

A man stands before a mirror, yet the mirror repeats his back instead of the reflection of his face.

The precise pictorial logic makes the impossibility especially convincing: the violation occurs not in the technique but in the very arrangement of the image.

16. Time Transfixed

In Time Transfixed of 1938 a locomotive emerges from a fireplace in a neat bourgeois interior.

The chimney and the steam engine are linked by the function of smoke, but their scale and their spatial conjunction remain impossible.

The picture was part of Magritte's relations with Edward James and shows how a commission could become a laboratory of a poetical juxtaposition.

17. The title as a collective practice

Magritte not infrequently discussed the titles of the pictures with the friends during the meetings of the Belgian Surrealist group.

Paul Nougé, Louis Scutenaire and other participants proposed titles that did not explain the image but created an additional tension.

The title should therefore be treated as a separate element of the work and at times as the result of a collective authorship.

18. Photography, staging and the home theatre

Magritte and his circle created staged photographs in which they performed gestures, disguises, doubles and visual jokes.

Photography served not only as documentation but as an autonomous field of Surrealist action.

The domestic scale of these scenes differed from the heroic model of the avant-garde artist and stressed the collective, playful side of the practice.

19. The war and "Surrealism in full sunlight"

During and after the German occupation Magritte temporarily changed his palette and turned to a lighter, more painterly manner.

He called this direction "Surrealism in full sunlight", opposing it to the sombre atmosphere of the wartime.

The period remains contentious: an optimistic painting could be at once an aesthetic resistance and a withdrawal from the direct depiction of political violence.

20. The vache period

In 1948 for an exhibition in Paris Magritte created a series of deliberately coarse, bright and caricatural works.

The name "période vache" is linked to a mocking aggressiveness, a cheap paint and a refusal of the habitual refinement of his images.

This episode was an attack on the Parisian taste and at the same time a demonstration of the artist's ability to destroy his own brand.

21. Golconda and the anonymous man in the bowler hat

In Golconda of 1953 a multitude of almost identical men in overcoats and bowler hats hang before the façades of houses.

The figures can be read as a rain, a crowd, a repeating social type or a scattered self-portrait.

Magritte turns the standard bourgeois suit into a sign of anonymity, repetition and an anxious impossibility of defining the space.

22. The Empire of Light and repetition

In the Empire of Light cycle a daytime sky is joined with a nocturnal street, house or landscape.

The contradiction does not look fantastic because of the restrained painterly manner and the precise light.

Magritte repeatedly returned to the composition, creating versions for different patrons; the repetition became at once an investigation and an economic model.

23. The Son of Man and the late visual canon

The Son of Man of 1964 shows a bowler-hatted man whose face is hidden by a green apple.

The picture is linked to the enduring motif of the visible that promises to reveal a personality but is immediately blocked by a new object.

Later the image became one of the most commercially recognisable symbols of twentieth-century art, often detached from the original philosophical problem.

24. Sculpture, film and the widening of the media

In the late period Magritte translated some of the pictorial motifs into bronze sculpture.

He also made short amateur films with the friends and the family, using masks, domestic spaces and staged actions.

These works show that his method did not depend solely on the painted canvas and could be transferred between media.

25. Georgette Magritte, the friends and collective production

Georgette Magritte was a constant model, the keeper of the home studio, a participant in the photographic stagings and the guardian of the artist's personal environment.

The friends proposed titles, wrote texts, took part in photographs, magazines and curatorial projects.

The recognition of the collective milieu does not cancel Magritte's authorship but makes the history of his works more precise and less mythologised.

26. Advertising, design and global recognisability

The clear outline, the single dominant object and the simple visual contradiction made Magritte's language especially influential for advertising, covers, cinema and the music industry.

Commercial culture often uses the apple, the bowler hat, the cloud and the covered face as ready-made signs of a "Surrealist" quality.

Such a spread confirms the strength of the method but at the same time turns a critical riddle into a decorative brand.

27. Critique: women, the colonial and the commodity image

The female body in Magritte is not infrequently turned into a fragment, an object of substitution or the surface of a visual operation.

Like the European Surrealism as a whole, his milieu was formed within a society with colonial and gender hierarchies that were not always the object of a direct critique.

A contemporary reading must analyse not only the intellectual game but also whose body, labour and cultural experience are turned into the material of the image.

28. The museums, the Foundation and authentication

The Musée Magritte Museum in Brussels opened in 2009 on the basis of the collection of the Royal Museums of Fine Arts of Belgium.

The Fondation Magritte and the Comité Magritte work with the archive, the image rights and the questions of authentication.

The large number of repetitions, variants, graphic works and posthumous reproductions makes the verification of the provenance especially important for the market and for publication.

29. Conclusion

René Magritte made the everyday image a philosophical tool.

His pictures show that sight, language and the name do not coincide automatically and that a realist technique can produce a radical uncertainty.

Magritte's chief legacy is not a set of apples, pipes and bowler hats but a discipline of doubt about how the image creates reality.
