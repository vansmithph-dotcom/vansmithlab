Publication passport

Working title

Andy Warhol: from commercial illustration and Campbell's Soup Cans to the Factory, Interview, television and The Last Supper

Editorial thesis

Andy Warhol's significance rests not only on the transfer of advertising and consumer goods into the museum. He changed the very model of artistic production, joining seriality, mechanical reproduction, collective labour, celebrity, media and business.

Reader question

How did an artist from a working-class family in Pittsburgh turn the surface of mass culture into one of the most influential visual languages of the twentieth century — and what price did authorship, intimacy and the people around the Factory pay for it?

Material boundaries

The primary object is Andy Warhol: his painting, graphic art, cinema, photography, publishing and television projects.

The Factory is treated as a collective production environment, not only as a mythical party.

The work with others' images is analysed through authorship, rights, repetition and commercial culture.

The collaborators, performers, models and Superstars are not dissolved in the sole authorship of the artist.

The trauma after the 1968 attack is treated without sensationalism.

Museum images and reproductions of works require a separate rights review.

Short answer

Andy Warhol was born Andrew Warhola in Pittsburgh on 6 August 1928. In 1949 he graduated from the Carnegie Institute of Technology in pictorial design and moved to New York, where he became a successful commercial illustrator.

In the early 1960s Warhol entered the history of Pop Art with the series Campbell's Soup Cans, Marilyn, Elvis, Coca-Cola and Death and Disaster. He then widened his practice to cinema, music, publishing, television, photography and the management of his own celebrity. He died in New York on 22 February 1987.

1. Pittsburgh, family and early visual culture

Andrew Warhola was born in a working-class district of Pittsburgh into a family of Carpatho-Rusyn emigrants, Andrej and Julia Warhola. The family preserved the Byzantine Catholic religious tradition.

In childhood an illness kept him at home for a long time. Film magazines, photographs of stars and drawing became an important part of his visual world.

The combination of the religious icon, the Hollywood portrait and the mass print later showed itself in the serial images of celebrities.

2. Carnegie Tech and commercial illustration

From 1945 to 1949 Warhol studied at the Carnegie Institute of Technology, now Carnegie Mellon University, and gained a degree in pictorial design.

The study joined drawing, design, print graphics and professional communication.

After graduating he moved to New York, where he quickly entered the world of magazines, fashion, window displays and advertising.

3. New York of the 1950s

In the 1950s Warhol worked for magazines, department stores and brands, including fashion and jewellery companies.

His recognisable blotted-line technique allowed a drawing that was at once manual and reproducible.

Commercial success gave him an understanding of the commission, the print run, packaging and visual positioning — skills that later became part of his artistic strategy.

4. The transition from advertising to Pop Art

By the start of the 1960s Warhol had begun to use images from newspapers, comics, commodities and celebrities.

He rejected the idea of the unique gesture as the sole proof of artistic authenticity.

The mass image became not a theme of a painting but a ready module that could be enlarged, repeated and produced in series.

5. Campbell's Soup Cans

In 1962 Warhol showed at the Ferus Gallery in Los Angeles a series of 32 images of Campbell's Soup cans — one for each variety of soup then in existence.

An ordinary commodity was presented as a row of almost identical images, at once reminiscent of a shop shelf and a museum series.

The work changed the attitude to advertising, packaging and repetition, making everyday consumption a subject of artistic analysis.

6. Marilyn Diptych and the production of an icon

After the death of Marilyn Monroe in 1962 Warhol began using her publicity photograph as the basis for serigraphs.

The Marilyn Diptych joins bright colour repetitions and gradually fading black-and-white impressions.

Celebrity becomes at once a commodity, a religious icon, a media ghost and an image of death.

7. Death and Disaster

In the Death and Disaster series Warhol turned to newspaper images of accidents, suicides, the electric chair and racial conflict.

Repetition dulls the shock, but at the same time shows how the mass press turns trauma into a consumable picture.

The series shatters the idea of Pop Art as exclusively a bright and carefree art.

8. Serigraphy, repetition and error

Photographic serigraphy allowed a found image to be transferred repeatedly to a canvas.

Displacements, gaps of colour and uneven printing were not always corrected: they became part of the work.

The process combined mechanical reproduction and physical chance, making each repetition similar but not identical.

9. Brillo Boxes and the boundary of art

In 1964 Warhol exhibited wooden boxes reproducing the packaging of Brillo and other consumer goods.

The objects looked almost like warehouse containers, although they were produced for the gallery.

The work raised the question of what distinguishes an artistic object from industrial packaging: material, context, authorship or an institutional decision.

10. The Factory as a production system

The Factory was at once a studio, a film set, an office, a social scene and a place of image production.

Paintings, films, photographs, music, publications and events were created there with the participation of assistants, visitors and performers.

The name emphasised an industrial model of art, but the real work depended on a complex network of people, often insufficiently noted in traditional attribution.

11. Superstars, labour and vulnerability

The Factory created its own system of celebrities — Superstars whose appearance and behaviour became the content of films and of a public myth.

Edie Sedgwick, Candy Darling, Jackie Curtis, Holly Woodlawn, Viva and many others gained a visibility rarely accessible outside this milieu.

At the same time the boundaries between participation, exploitation, friendship and labour remained blurred, and the consequences for individual people could be severe.

12. Screen Tests

Between 1964 and 1966 Warhol shot roughly 500 short screen portraits.

The subject was asked to stay almost motionless in front of a stationary 16mm camera; when projected the film was slowed down.

The standardised procedure turned minimal gestures, blinking and facial tension into a dramatic event.

13. Sleep, Empire and duration

The early films Sleep and Empire radically stretch screen time and reduce the number of events.

The camera records a sleeping person or a motionless skyscraper, forcing the viewer to become aware of his own attention.

Duration becomes not a technical parameter but the main material of the film.

14. Chelsea Girls and the expanded film image

The Chelsea Girls of 1966 consists of separate long episodes shown in pairs on two screens.

Scenes featuring Factory participants form not a linear plot but a changeable system of adjacencies, sound and chance.

The film joined experimental cinema, portrait, performance and the documentation of an urban subculture.

15. The Velvet Underground and Exploding Plastic Inevitable

In the mid-1960s Warhol became the manager and producer of The Velvet Underground, linking the band to the multimedia performances of Exploding Plastic Inevitable.

Film projections, stroboscopic light, dance and loud music created an environment in which art could not be divided into separate disciplines.

For the debut album The Velvet Underground & Nico, Warhol designed the famous banana cover and supported the group's creative autonomy.

16. Most Wanted Men and censorship

For the New York State pavilion at the 1964 World's Fair Warhol created a façade work Thirteen Most Wanted Men.

Portraits of wanted men were deemed too confrontational for the optimistic image of the fair and were soon painted over.

The project showed the limits of the permissible image when mass visual culture comes up against state representation.

17. The 1968 attack

On 3 June 1968 Valerie Solanas shot Warhol at the Union Square studio. He underwent many hours of surgery and a lengthy recovery.

After the attack his body, his work routine and his attitude to an open studio environment changed.

The trauma strengthened the themes already present in his art of the body's surface, vulnerability, medical technology and death.

18. Interview Magazine

In 1969 Warhol launched Interview as a monthly film magazine.

Over time the publication became a chronicle of art, fashion, music, society, celebrity and night life.

A recorded conversation of a celebrity with a celebrity turned the interview into part of a network of mutual promotion and media production.

19. The commissioned portrait and Business Art

In the 1970s and 1980s Warhol developed a profitable system of commissioned portraits of politicians, collectors, actors and businessmen.

A Polaroid snapshot became the source material for a serigraph, and the individual image was inserted into a standardised production scheme.

The notion of Business Art describes not the departure of art into commerce but the turning of the business operation itself into an artistic medium.

20. Polaroid, photography and television

Warhol constantly photographed people, parties, travels and his own everyday life with a Polaroid and compact cameras.

In his late period he created the television programmes Fashion, Andy Warhol's T.V. and Andy Warhol's Fifteen Minutes.

Television continued his interest in conversation, celebrity, fashion and the repeatable public persona.

21. Time Capsules

From 1974 Warhol began to place the correspondence, photographs, newspapers, invitations, bills and random objects on his desk into boxes that were then sent into storage.

The museum archive counts 569 standard Time Capsule boxes, as well as additional containers.

An everyday method of clearing the desk turned into the largest serial work and a material archive of a cultural network.

22. Basquiat, Haring and the late collaborations

In the 1980s Warhol worked with younger artists, including Jean-Michel Basquiat and Keith Haring.

The joint paintings of Warhol and Basquiat combined logos, serigraphic signs, drawing and the painterly gesture.

The collaborations stirred debate about power, the market and mutual influence but also shattered the myth of the late Warhol as a wholly isolated brand.

23. Religion and The Last Supper

Warhol's religious life long remained less visible than his public image of a secular observer.

In the late works he repeatedly turned to the cross, the Last Supper and images of Catholic culture.

The Last Supper series, shown in Milan at the start of 1987, joins Leonardo, advertising, repetition and a personal religious memory.

24. Authorship, assistants and production

Warhol deliberately blurred the boundary between individual authorship and collective production.

Gerard Malanga, Billy Name, Brigid Berlin, Paul Morrissey, Vincent Fremont, Ronnie Cutrone and many others took part in the creation of images, films, events and business infrastructure.

The history of the Factory requires a precise attribution of roles, not an automatic transfer of all the work to the name of the central celebrity.

25. Critique: surface, consumption and ethics

Warhol has been criticised for superficiality, cynicism, market-mindedness and the turning of people into material for his own brand.

On the other hand, it was precisely the refusal of an explicatory depth that allowed him to show the power of packaging, media and the repeatable image.

An ethical assessment must take into account not only the formal innovation but also the relations of labour, consent, vulnerability and commercial gain.

26. The foundation, the museum and the posthumous legacy

After Warhol's death in 1987, in accordance with his will, The Andy Warhol Foundation for the Visual Arts was created to support the visual arts.

The Andy Warhol Museum opened in Pittsburgh in 1994 and holds the largest collection of the artist's works and archival materials.

The foundation continues grant-making, licensing and the support of the scholarly catalogue raisonné, turning the posthumous brand into a resource for new artists.

27. Conclusion

Andy Warhol changed not only the set of permissible artistic images but the very organisation of artistic work.

He showed that the commodity, the portrait, the magazine, the film, the studio and the business can act as interconnected media.

Warhol's main legacy is an understanding of culture as a factory of images in which the viewer is at once consumer, participant and product.
