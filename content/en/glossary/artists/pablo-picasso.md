Working title

Pablo Picasso: from the Blue Period and Les Demoiselles d'Avignon to Cubism, Guernica, ceramics and the global art market

Editorial thesis

Picasso's significance is grounded in a radical widening of the means of depiction, material and authorial identity. Yet his canonical status should be examined together with the contribution of Georges Braque, the artists of African and Iberian cultures, the labour of the workshops, the relations with women and the institutional economy of modernism.

Reader question

How was an artist able repeatedly to destroy his own style, remain the central figure of twentieth-century art and at the same time create a biographical myth that still determines the market, the museums and the popular idea of genius?

Material boundaries

The primary object is Pablo Picasso: his painting, graphic art, sculpture, ceramics, scenography and public image.

Cubism is treated as a joint development of Picasso and Georges Braque, not as an individual invention.

The influence of African and Iberian objects is analysed together with the colonial context of European collections.

The artist's relations with women are examined without a romanticisation of the "muse" concept.

Guernica is analysed as a political work, a museum object and an international symbol.

The mass market, editions and copyright are separated from the artistic assessment.

Short answer

Pablo Ruiz Picasso was born in Málaga in 1881. He studied in La Coruña, Barcelona and Madrid, and at the beginning of the twentieth century linked his life to Paris. His path includes the Blue and Rose periods, Cubism, Neoclassicism, Surrealist experiments, a political painting and a late expressive manner.

Les Demoiselles d'Avignon, the joint work with Braque on Cubism and Guernica became the central points of the history of modernism. Picasso also worked with sculpture, collage, engraving, ceramics, theatre and photography, shaping one of the most wide-ranging artistic practices of the twentieth century.

1. Málaga, La Coruña and family training

Pablo Picasso was born on 25 October 1881 in Málaga. His father, José Ruiz Blasco, was a painter, a drawing teacher and a museum worker.

The early training was academic: life drawing, the copying of plaster casts, composition and technical discipline.

The family's moves to La Coruña and Barcelona gave Picasso access to different art schools and an urban culture.

2. Barcelona, Els Quatre Gats and early modernism

In Barcelona Picasso entered the circle of artists, writers and anarchist-minded youth around the café Els Quatre Gats.

He quickly mastered the stylistics of modernism, the graphics of the magazine and the language of the Paris poster.

The early exhibitions and drawings show that his artistic identity was formed through competition, exchange and the observation of urban life.

3. Paris and the Blue Period

His first trips to Paris began in 1900. The death of Carles Casagemas became one of the emotional sources of the Blue Period.

The paintings of 1901–1904 use a cold palette, elongated figures and the themes of poverty, solitude, illness and social exclusion.

These images created a strong emotional language but also turned poverty into an aesthetic object for the art market.

4. The Rose Period and the image of the artist

In 1904 Picasso settled permanently in Paris and worked at the Bateau-Lavoir.

The Rose Period is linked to circus performers, acrobats, harlequins and a warmer palette.

The figure of the performer becomes an autobiographical double: at once a marginal figure, a professional and a participant in a commercial spectacle.

5. Iberian sculpture and the colonial museum

In 1906–1907 Picasso studied ancient Iberian sculpture and objects presented in Parisian ethnographic collections.

The simplified faces, frontality and dense plasticity influenced his departure from a naturalistic depiction.

These contacts took place within the European museum system, shaped by colonial extraction and an unequal classification of cultures.

6. Les Demoiselles d'Avignon

Les Demoiselles d'Avignon was created in 1907 after a large number of drawings and compositional changes.

Five figures directly face the viewer, and the space is split into sharp planes without a stable perspective.

The picture became a key work of modernism, but its brothel theme, the mask-like faces and the aggressive gaze require an analysis of gender, sexuality and the colonial imagination.

7. Cubism with Georges Braque

After 1907 Picasso and Georges Braque began a close exchange of ideas, motifs and technical solutions.

Their collaboration was so intense that individual works are sometimes hard to distinguish without signatures and archival data.

Cubism should therefore be described as a joint investigation rather than as the achievement of a single artist.

8. Analytical Cubism

In Analytical Cubism the object is broken down into intersecting planes and shown as a sum of different points of view.

The palette becomes limited, and the distinction between figure and ground weakens.

The image does not simply record a visible object; it shows the process of its intellectual construction.

9. Collage and Synthetic Cubism

From 1912 Picasso and Braque began to include newspaper, wallpaper, typography and imitations of materials in their works.

Collage broke the boundary between the depiction of an object and a fragment of everyday reality itself.

Synthetic Cubism made the form flatter, brighter and more emblematic, opening the way for design, typography and mass visual culture.

10. African art, appropriation and the canon

In the European history of modernism, African objects were long described as an anonymous source of inspiration.

Such a language hides the authorship of specific cultures, the ritual function of the objects and the colonial conditions of their removal.

A contemporary analysis must at once recognise the formal influence and criticise the system that assigned the objects the status of raw material for the European avant-garde.

11. The Ballets Russes and scenography

In 1917 Picasso worked with Sergei Diaghilev's Ballets Russes on the ballet Parade.

The project joined painting, costume, set, the music of Erik Satie and the choreography of Léonide Massine.

The theatre widened his interest in scale, movement, costume and the collective production of the image.

12. The Neoclassical period

After the First World War, in parallel with his Cubist works, Picasso turned to large, heavy and clear human figures.

The Neoclassical manner was not a rejection of the avant-garde but part of a strategy of the simultaneous existence of several languages.

This shatters the idea of his oeuvre as a linear sequence of styles.

13. Surrealist plasticity and the Minotaur

In the 1920s–1930s the bodies in Picasso's works become tense, dismembered and metamorphic.

The image of the Minotaur links sexuality, violence, blindness, the artist and a mythological power.

The Surrealist milieu strengthened Picasso's interest in the unconscious, but the artist kept his independence from the group.

14. Sculpture and construction

Picasso worked with sculpture throughout his life, using clay, wood, metal, plaster, found objects and assembled constructions.

The Guitar of 1912 changed the idea of sculpture: the volume was not carved from a mass but assembled from planes.

Later, the work at Boisgeloup and the collaboration with Julio González widened his metallic and spatial practice.

15. The Vollard Suite and print graphics

The Vollard Suite is a series of one hundred etchings created in the 1930s.

It includes the themes of the sculptor's studio, the artist and the model, the Minotaur, violence and classical mythology.

Print graphics allowed Picasso to work in series, to repeat a motif and to distribute the image beyond the unique picture.

16. Dora Maar and the process of creating Guernica

Dora Maar documented the stages of the creation of Guernica in the studio on Rue des Grands-Augustins.

Her photographs give a rare record of the successive changes in the composition.

Maar was an independent artist and photographer; the reduction of her role to the biographical category of a "muse" distorts the professional significance of her work.

17. Guernica

Guernica was painted in 1937 for the pavilion of the Spanish Republic at the International Exhibition in Paris.

The cause was the bombing of the Basque town of Guernica by German and Italian air forces supporting Franco's forces.

The monochrome composition joins the figures of people, a bull, a horse and a destroyed space, turning a specific event into an image of violence against a civilian population.

18. War, France and a political position

Picasso remained in France during the German occupation and continued to work in Paris.

His art was not always direct propaganda, yet Guernica and the linked works shaped an enduring anti-war image.

The artist's political reputation was made up of works, public statements, exhibitions and the use of his images by different movements.

19. The Communist Party and the image of the public intellectual

In 1944 Picasso joined the French Communist Party.

He took part in peace congresses and created images linked to the anti-war movement, including the dove of peace.

His relations with the party were complex: the symbolic authority of the artist did not always coincide with the demands of Socialist Realism.

20. Vallauris and ceramics

After the war Picasso worked actively in Vallauris with the Madoura workshop.

He created dishes, jugs, vases and sculptural forms, using the surface of the vessel as a space for drawing.

Ceramics widened his practice but also depended on the technical knowledge of Suzanne and Georges Ramié and the labour of the workshop.

21. Late painting and the dialogue with the Old Masters

In the later decades Picasso interpreted Velázquez, Delacroix, Manet and other masters.

The series after Las Meninas and Le Déjeuner sur l'herbe turn the canon into material for repetition and variation.

The late manner is distinguished by speed, the energy of line, an overt corporeality and a resistance to the idea of a quiet old age.

22. Women, power and the myth of the muse

Fernande Olivier, Eva Gouel, Olga Khokhlova, Marie-Thérèse Walter, Dora Maar, Françoise Gilot and Jacqueline Roque are linked to different periods of his life and art.

Popular history often turns them into a succession of "muses", erasing their autonomy, professional work and the asymmetry of power.

A contemporary editorial position must take into account the evidence of psychological violence, control and inequality, without reducing the works to biography.

23. The studio and collective production

The scale of Picasso's legacy was impossible without lithographers, ceramists, printers, photographers, secretaries, gallerists and conservators.

The Picasso signature ensured the market value, while the production labour often remained less visible.

A correct attribution must distinguish the authorial conception, the making, the reproduction and the posthumous management of the legacy.

24. Photography, cinema and the public image

Picasso constantly interacted with photographers and film-makers, including Brassaï, Robert Doisneau, Lucien Clergue and Henri-Georges Clouzot.

The film Le Mystère Picasso turned the process of drawing into a public spectacle.

The camera supported the image of the artist as an indefatigable creator and became an important part of the production of his myth.

25. Museums, the legacy and the art market

After Picasso's death in 1973 his legacy became the basis of major museum collections, research, exhibitions and legal structures.

The Musée Picasso Paris holds a substantial part of the legacy received by the state, and the Museu Picasso Barcelona is particularly important for the study of the artist's early years.

High prices, a large number of editioned works and the popularity of the name create constant risks of a false attribution, forgeries and a simplified marketing.

26. Conclusion

Picasso widened the boundaries of painting, sculpture, collage, print graphics and artistic identity.

His influence is inexplicable through a single style: it rests on a constant reworking of forms, history, media and his own biography.

A critical understanding of Picasso demands that one at once recognise the artistic power, the collective sources, the political context and the inequality of personal power.
