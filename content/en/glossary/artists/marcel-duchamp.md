Working title

Marcel Duchamp: from Nude Descending a Staircase and the readymade to The Large Glass, Rrose Sélavy and Étant donnés

Editorial thesis

Marcel Duchamp's significance rests on a consistent re-examination of what makes an object a work of art. He replaced the idea of unique skill with a system of choice, language, reproducibility, chance and the viewer's participation, creating the foundation for conceptual art, institutional critique and contemporary curatorial thinking.

Reader question

How did one artist manage to change twentieth-century art by almost giving up traditional painting, turning the ready-made object, the signature, the reproduction and the rules of the game into full artistic instruments?

Material boundaries

The primary object is Marcel Duchamp: his works, texts, curatorial projects, chess practice and network of collaborators.

The readymade is treated not as a simple provocation but as a system of selection, context and authorisation.

The dispute about the authorship of Fountain and the role of Elsa von Freytag-Loringhoven is noted as a discussion not definitively closed.

Rrose Sélavy is analysed as an authorial persona and a linguistic mechanism, not only as a mask or costume.

Étant donnés is examined together with questions of voyeurism, gender, the power of the gaze and the museum installation.

Images of the works are protected by the rights of the Estate of Marcel Duchamp / ARS / ADAGP and require separate licensing.

Short answer

Marcel Duchamp was born in 1887 in Blainville-Crevon in France. After his early work with Cubism and a Futurist decomposition of movement, he rejected the traditional idea of the artist as the producer of painterly objects.

Nude Descending a Staircase No. 2, Bicycle Wheel, Fountain, The Large Glass, L.H.O.O.Q., Boîte-en-valise and Étant donnés form not a sequence of styles but an investigation of authorship, the original, the replica, chance, language, desire and the museum context.

1. The Duchamp family and the artistic milieu

Marcel Duchamp was born in 1887 in Blainville-Crevon in Normandy. Several members of the family became artists: Jacques Villon, Raymond Duchamp-Villon and Suzanne Duchamp.

The family milieu linked the visual arts, music, literature and play. This interdisciplinarity later appeared in Duchamp's refusal to recognise rigid boundaries between genres.

He studied in Paris and early entered the artistic circles, yet held himself at a distance from the idea of a stable group or a single programme.

2. Early painting and the Puteaux group

In his early works Duchamp passed through Impressionist, Symbolist and Cubist influences.

He took part in the discussions of the Puteaux group, where artists were exploring geometry, movement, perception and the relations of art to science.

At the same time Duchamp quickly grew critical of the repetition of the Cubist language as a recognisable manner.

3. Nude Descending a Staircase No. 2

The 1912 painting joins the decomposition of form, the seriality of movement and a mechanical rhythm.

The work was rejected by the Cubist exhibition, since its dynamic theme and title did not match the group's expectations.

This episode widened Duchamp's distance from a collective stylistic discipline.

4. The Armory Show and the production of scandal

In 1913 Nude Descending a Staircase No. 2 was shown at the Armory Show in New York.

The picture became one of the chief objects of the public polemic about European modernism in the United States.

The scandal shaped Duchamp's image as a provocateur, although his subsequent work was directed rather toward a dismantling of familiar categories than a repetition of sensation.

5. From painting to the investigation of the conditions of art

After 1912 Duchamp was less and less interested in painting as the production of a "retinal" pleasure.

He sought to work with ideas, language, rules and the relations between the object and the viewer.

The rejection of a single style allowed him to use glass, ready-made objects, notes, reproductions, photography, film and the exhibition environment.

6. 3 Standard Stoppages and controlled chance

In 3 Standard Stoppages Duchamp dropped metre-long threads from a set height and recorded their randomly formed curves.

The work parodies the idea of a universal standard of measurement, replacing the straight metric unit with the individual contours of chance.

Chance here does not mean an absence of method: the artist sets the procedure, the conditions and the way of preserving the result.

7. Bicycle Wheel and the origin of the readymade

Bicycle Wheel of 1913 joins a bicycle wheel with a kitchen stool.

Duchamp later placed the work in the history of the readymade, although he had initially perceived the rotation of the wheel as a private visual pleasure.

The original object has not survived; the known museum versions are late authorised replicas.

8. The definition of the readymade

Duchamp introduced the term "readymade" in 1916 to designate a factory-made object chosen and designated by the artist.

The key operation becomes not the manual making but the decision: the choice of an object, the change of its mode of use, the title, the date, the signature and the context of the display.

The readymade does not cancel authorship but shifts it from the production of form to the production of the conditions of interpretation.

9. Fountain and the Society of Independent Artists

In 1917 a porcelain urinal, rotated and signed "R. Mutt 1917", was submitted to the exhibition of the Society of Independent Artists.

Despite the declared principle of the absence of a jury, the object was not shown. The original soon disappeared.

Fountain became the central example of the conflict between the institutional rules, the authorial choice and the definition of a work of art.

10. The original, the replica and the photograph

Most of the viewers know Fountain not through the lost original but through the photograph by Alfred Stieglitz, the magazine publication and the late replicas.

In the 1950s and 1960s Duchamp authorised the making of several full-size versions of the readymades.

Thus the replica is not a secondary compromise: it becomes part of the work about the instability of the original and of authorisation.

11. L.H.O.O.Q. and an intervention in the canon

In 1919 Duchamp added a moustache and beard to a reproduction of the Mona Lisa and gave the work the phonetically ambiguous title L.H.O.O.Q.

A minimal intervention turns a mass reproduction of a museum icon into a new work.

The work joins parody, a gender ambiguity, a linguistic play and a critique of cultural authority.

12. The Large Glass

The Bride Stripped Bare by Her Bachelors, Even was created between 1915 and 1923 on two large glass panels.

The upper zone belongs to the "Bride", the lower to a system of "Bachelors", machines, forms and processes that do not reach a union.

The work unites drawing, painting, a mechanical metaphor, an erotic drama, perspective and the real effects of transparency.

13. The machine of desire, the text and the incompleteness

The Large Glass does not offer a single narrative: the viewer assembles the relations between the images, the terms and the notes.

In 1923 Duchamp declared the work definitively unfinished, making the incompleteness part of its status.

After the glass was damaged in transport he included the network of cracks in the work, accepting chance as an additional co-author.

14. The Green Box and the work as an archive

In 1934 Duchamp issued the Green Box — a box of facsimile notes, diagrams and materials connected to The Large Glass.

The box does not explain the work definitively but widens it into a network of documents, puns and technical speculations.

Thus the work exists at once as an object, a text, a process, an archive and an instruction for interpretation.

15. Rrose Sélavy

In the 1920s Duchamp created the authorial persona Rrose Sélavy; her name is linked to French phonetic games.

Man Ray photographed Duchamp in the guise of Rrose, and the name appeared as the signature of a publisher, an author and an owner of the works.

The persona unsettles the stability of gender, name and authorship, but it should be analysed in its historical context without automatically attributing to it a contemporary identity.

16. Optical machines and Anémic Cinéma

Duchamp created rotating devices in which the geometric patterns produce an illusion of depth when set in motion.

In the 1926 film Anémic Cinéma the rotating discs alternate with circular inscriptions and sexualised puns.

The optical becomes a mechanical and a linguistic system in which the visible constantly passes into the legible.

17. Chess as an intellectual practice

From the 1920s Duchamp devoted a substantial part of his time to chess, took part in tournaments and wrote about chess problems.

Chess allowed him to join strict rules, calculation, an aesthetic decision and the unpredictability of a position.

Its role should not be understood as a simple abandonment of art: a chess mindset is linked to his interest in procedure and the hidden structure.

18. The Société Anonyme and the work with collections

In 1920 Duchamp, together with Katherine Dreier and Man Ray, founded the Société Anonyme.

The organisation staged exhibitions, lectures and collected works of modern art, forming an important educational infrastructure in the United States.

Duchamp also helped the collectors Louise and Walter Arensberg and later took part in the transfer of their collection to the Philadelphia Museum of Art.

19. Curatorial design and Surrealism

Duchamp took part in creating the exhibition environment for the Surrealist exhibitions, turning the installation into an independent artistic tool.

In 1938 the ceiling of the Paris exhibition was filled with sacks, and in 1942 the space of the New York show was crossed by a network of strings.

Such solutions complicated the movement and the visibility, making the viewer a participant in a conflicted environment rather than a neutral observer.

20. Boîte-en-valise

The Boîte-en-valise, created from the end of the 1930s, presents a portable archive of miniature reproductions of Duchamp's works.

The artist turned his own legacy into a mobile museum that can be copied, assembled and transported.

The work questions the distinction between the original, the reproduction, the catalogue, the collection and the autobiography.

21. The replicas and the late publicity

In the 1950s and 1960s Duchamp gained a new publicity through lectures, interviews, retrospectives and publications.

The authorised replicas made the lost or inaccessible part of his legacy visible once again.

The late strategy shows that the preservation of a work can include a managed reproduction, not only the guarding of a single object.

22. Étant donnés

Étant donnés was created secretly from 1946 to 1966 and was installed in the Philadelphia Museum of Art after the artist's death.

The viewer looks through two holes in a wooden door at a diorama of a naked body, a landscape, a waterfall and a lamp.

The work brings back a figurative illusion and a material skill, while at the same time turning the very act of viewing into a rigidly controlled procedure.

23. The gaze, the body and an ethical critique

Étant donnés raises questions of voyeurism, access, the violence of the gaze and the depiction of the female body.

The viewer cannot enter the space or change the angle: the construction fixes the relations between the observing subject and the displayed body.

A contemporary analysis must take into account not only the enigmatic quality of the work but also the gender asymmetry of its visual regime.

24. Authorship, co-authorship and disputed attributions

Duchamp's works depended on photographers, publishers, the makers of replicas, collectors, curators and museum specialists.

The history of Fountain includes a continuing debate about the possible role of Elsa von Freytag-Loringhoven; the available documents allow different interpretations and do not permit the question to be presented as fully resolved.

A correct history must distinguish the conceptual authorship, the making, the documentation, the publication and the institutional authorisation.

25. The influence on contemporary art

The readymade influenced conceptual art, Minimalism, Fluxus, institutional critique, appropriation art and practices based on the instruction.

Duchamp changed the role of the viewer: interpretation becomes not an addition but part of the production of the work.

His influence is especially visible where art works with rules, the archive, the replica, the context and the systems of display.

26. The current museum infrastructure

The Philadelphia Museum of Art holds the largest complex of Duchamp's works, including The Large Glass and Étant donnés.

The Duchamp Research Portal unites the digitised archives of the Philadelphia Museum of Art, the Centre Pompidou and the Association Marcel Duchamp and contains tens of thousands of images of documents and museum objects.

In 2026 MoMA and the Philadelphia Museum of Art are holding a major joint retrospective, once again examining Duchamp's career as a single system of works, documents, replicas and exhibitions.

27. Conclusion

Marcel Duchamp changed art by showing that a work is defined not only by its form and technique but by its choice, its name, its context, its rules and the viewer's action.

His practice destroyed the stable distinction between the original and the copy, the object and the document, the artist and the curator, art and an intellectual game.

Duchamp's chief legacy is not a ready formula of provocation but the demand that each time we check afresh who, where and on what grounds calls something art.
