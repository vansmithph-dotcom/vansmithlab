# Citation, copying, and archive — when repetition becomes new design

*Repetition becomes new design not through a change of surface, but when the system of access, the context, the authorship, and the function of an object change.*

## 1. Thesis: difference is made by position, not form

Two visually identical objects can be different things if their position in a system differs: who the author is, who is licensed to produce, whom it is for, what has been said about it. Form is not the only carrier of difference.

## 2. Four different operations

The word "repetition" hides four distinct operations: citation, reproduction, reissue, and counterfeit. What distinguishes them is not resemblance to the original but declaration. Citation acknowledges its source; counterfeit conceals it.

## 3. Citation: the source is named

Citation works only through recognition. It counts on the viewer knowing the original and builds meaning from the distance between source and new use. Without recognition, a citation becomes an ordinary form.

## 4. Reproduction: a run without a claim

Reproduction copies an image without claiming to be the original. A museum postcard does not deceive: it presents itself as derivative and is therefore legitimate. The honesty of the carrier decides everything.

## 5. Reissue: the right is restored

A reissue of a historical model by the rights-holding manufacturer creates a legitimate new object. It is not a copy but a continuation of production — and the difference is legal rather than visual.

## 6. Counterfeit: a concealed claim

Counterfeit differs in exactly one respect: it asserts an origin it does not have. Its harm is informational rather than aesthetic — it destroys the possibility of citing an object.

## 7. The readymade: transfer without alteration

The readymade showed the limit case: the object does not change at all, only the context of presentation does. If an institution can create difference without a single physical change, then difference is certainly not located in form.

## 8. Pop art: the run as content

Pop art made reproducibility a subject rather than a means. A work about the mass image must be mass in form, or it contradicts its own claim. Here copying became an argument.

## 9. Postmodernism legalised citation

Postmodern design brought ornament and historical form back inside quotation marks. Citation made it possible to use what modernism had rejected without abandoning contemporaneity — and the device has stayed in use.

## 10. The archive became a production tool

Fashion houses turned their own archives into a source of product. Reissuing a model from the archive is at once a historical gesture and a commercial decision, and the two usually cannot be separated.

## 11. Access changes meaning

The digitisation of collections changed the status of repetition. When an original is available to anyone at high resolution, copying stops being a privilege and becomes an ordinary operation — and value shifts to selection and explanation.

## 12. Function as differentiator

The same object changes type when its purpose changes. A chair designed for a pavilion and its domestic version are different objects in their requirements, though the silhouette matches. Function separates things as effectively as form.

## 13. Material as differentiator

Translating a form into another material creates a new object even when the outline matches exactly. A chair in plywood and the same chair in plastic have different behaviour, service life, and production logic.

## 14. Scale as differentiator

A change of scale changes the type of problem. An enlarged detail becomes furniture; a reduced building becomes an object. Proportion does not scale linearly, which makes the transfer a substantive operation.

## 15. Learning was always copying

Copying a model is the basic method of instruction in every discipline, from handwriting drills to redrawing someone else’s layout. The problem arises not in copying but in publishing a copy as an original.

## 16. First turn: reproducibility became normal

Industrial production made the run the ordinary condition of an object. From that point "original" stopped meaning a single example and came to mean the first authorised version.

## 17. Second turn: citation became a legitimate device

Recognising citation as a legitimate method freed designers from the demand for novelty at any cost. Assessment shifted from "is this new" to "why this particular source".

## 18. Third turn: the archive became public

Open digital collections made sources available to everyone simultaneously. This devalued the rarity of knowledge and raised the price of accurate attribution — knowing a source is no longer enough; it must be named correctly.

## 19. What is usually misunderstood

The error is to treat a percentage of change as the test of legitimacy. Legal and ethical boundaries are set by the claim of origin and by rights, not by the share of altered surface.

## 20. Counterargument: everything already existed

The familiar objection: originality is impossible, every form has a predecessor, and distinguishing citation from copy is wordplay serving rights holders.

## 21. Where the counterargument holds

It holds as a description of the creative process. Nobody works from zero, and the demand for absolute novelty is dishonest about how design is actually made.

## 22. Where it fails

It fails as an excuse. The difference between acknowledged and concealed borrowing is practical rather than philosophical: it determines whether a reader can verify a claim of origin.

## 23. A test: is the source named

The only working test is declaration. Is the source named, are rights stated, is the new version distinguishable from the original in documentation. Everything else is a matter of taste; this is a matter of verifiability.

## 26. What this changes in practice

The practical conclusion: record the source while working, not after publication. A reference made in advance turns borrowing into method; one made under pressure turns it into an excuse.

## 27. What stays with the original

The original keeps what cannot be reproduced: its date, the circumstances of its appearance, and its role in a history of decisions. A copy can repeat a form but cannot occupy a place in a chronology.

## 28. The current state

The speed of circulation has made attribution fragile: an image travels faster than its caption. Systems for confirming provenance have therefore become an infrastructural task rather than a formality.

## 29. Editorial conclusion

Repetition becomes new design when the verifiable position of an object changes: rights, purpose, material, scale, or declared source. A change of surface alone produces nothing.

## 30. How to read the subject today

On meeting a familiar form, ask not "where have I seen this" but "has it been stated where this was before". The presence of an answer separates work from appropriation.
