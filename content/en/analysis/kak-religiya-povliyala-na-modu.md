# How Religion
Influenced the World of Fashion

Author: Ivan Melnik

Type: signed analysis

Date of preparation: 5 August 2026

Status: detailed editorial document / draft for evidence review

Language: en • source_revision: 1

"Clothing becomes fashion not at the moment it ceases to be a necessity, but when society begins to read it as a system of signs."

Publication passport

Editorial thesis

Religion influenced fashion not only by prescribing how the body should be covered. It created visual systems — forms of clothing, materials, colours, symbols, rituals and hierarchies. Contemporary fashion inherited this language but has often separated it from faith, turning sacred codes into aesthetic, political and commercial images.

Reader question

In what way did religious beliefs, institutions and rituals influence the form of clothing, attitudes to the body, craft and the visual language of contemporary fashion?

Editorial promise

To show religion not as a simple set of prohibitions but as one of the most enduring systems that at once regulates clothing and creates forms, materials, symbols, crafts and modes of expressing identity.

Material boundaries

The material does not attempt to give an exhaustive history of all the world's religions.

Religious norms are examined in their historical and regional context, without claims about a uniform practice among all believers.

Ethnic, national and religious clothing are not automatically conflated.

The author's conclusions are separated from verifiable historical facts.

Contemporary designer case studies are examined only where the link to a religious tradition is documented or clearly attributed by a museum, archive or the designer himself.

1. Introduction. Clothing between body, society and the sacred

Clothing has never been only a protection against the cold or a way of decorating the body. It communicates who stands before us, which group the person belongs to, what role he performs, whether he is admitted to a given ritual and what place he occupies in the social hierarchy. Religion added one more level to this language: the relationship between the body, morality, the community and the idea of the sacred.

Religious influence on fashion cannot therefore be reduced to prohibitions. Religious institutions did indeed regulate the visibility of the body, luxury and the differences between sexes or estates. But they also created the most complex systems of vestments, supported weaving, embroidery, the jeweller's craft and the production of ceremonial objects. Temples, courts and religious communities became patrons of textiles; rituals preserved certain forms of clothing for centuries; symbols of faith turned into ornament, accessory and a sign of status.

Fashion, by contrast, works through change. It takes a stable sign and places it in a new environment: the monastic hood becomes an element of an avant-garde silhouette; liturgical gold a code of luxury; the closed form the basis of contemporary modest fashion; the cross a piece of jewellery that can be confessional, cultural or merely decorative. In this transition the meaning does not disappear entirely. It becomes multilayered and sometimes conflicted.

It is more accurate to say not that religion "created fashion" but that it was one of the most durable forces that clothed clothing with meaning. Specific forms always arose at the intersection of faith, climate, materials, trade, politics, gender, class, colonialism and migration. This is especially important for a global history: there is no single universal Christian, Islamic, Jewish or Buddhist dress.

2. Seven mechanisms of religious influence

2.1 Regulation of the body

Religious norms determined which parts of the body should be covered, how hair was to be worn, whether a display of wealth was permissible and how clothing distinguished the everyday, prayer, mourning or festivity.

2.2 Creation of special types of clothing

The vestments of clergy, monastic dress, head coverings, ritual belts, wedding ensembles and pilgrimage clothing formed stable forms that survived political and stylistic change.

2.3 Visual language

Colour, gold, embroidery, calligraphy, geometry, vegetal ornament, precious stones and strict symmetry acquired religious functions and later entered secular design.

2.4 Identity

Clothing communicated belonging to a community, office, marital status, region, age and at times the degree of religious learning or discipline.

2.5 Craft and patronage

Temples, monasteries, courts and charitable foundations commissioned fabrics and jewellery, sustaining highly specialised crafts.

2.6 Conflict between norm and self-expression

Believers constantly interpreted the rules: they chose the fabric, the colour, the degree of decoration and the ways of combining modernity with tradition.

2.7 Secularisation of the sacred

Fashion extracts the religious image from its original context and turns it into a style, a commodity, a provocation or a means of cultural memory.

3. Modesty: not the absence of fashion but a different design of the body

In the popular imagination religious clothing is often associated above all with modesty. Yet modesty is not a single item of clothing and does not belong to a single religion. It is a changing system of norms that can concern length, transparency, closeness to the body, the hair, the neckline, the legs, the arms, jewellery, colour and even the manner of movement.

Christian writers of late antiquity discussed women's clothing, adornment and the covering of the head as questions of morality, status and behaviour. But even in the strictest texts clothing remained a means of action: through it women could demonstrate piety, family position or social dignity. European traditions of covering the head and face also had their own complex history and cannot be regarded as an exclusively Islamic phenomenon.

Islamic norms of modesty were formed in different legal, cultural and political contexts. The terms "hijab", "niqab", "chador" and "abaya" are not interchangeable: they designate different objects and practices. Their form depends on the region, the era, the local fashion and personal choice. For some women covered clothing is a religious obligation, for others a cultural norm, a sign of community, a political gesture or an aesthetic preference; not infrequently these meanings are combined.

From a design point of view, modest clothing does not rule out fashionability. On the contrary, the constraint creates a separate field of formal invention: the designer works with volume instead of exposure, with layering instead of transparency, with the surface of the fabric instead of a direct emphasis on the body. Drapery, movement, the proportion of the sleeve, the interaction of the head covering and the silhouette, the complexity of texture and colour combinations all become important.

Modesty is not the absence of a visual language. It is a language in which attractiveness is built through distance, the rhythm of the fabric, the control of the silhouette and a socially legible degree of coveredness.

Contemporary modest fashion has turned into a transregional market. Studies of Muslim fashion show how forms perceived as "authentic" in one region are adapted by designers and consumers in another. The abaya, for instance, may retain its religious function while at the same time taking part in the system of seasonal fashion, brands, influencers and luxury consumption. This creates internal disputes: when does aestheticisation strengthen a religious identity, and when does consumer logic dissolve the spiritual meaning?

4. Clothing as a sign of belonging

Religious clothing rarely communicates belief alone. It can at once point to origin, family status, profession, rank, age and local tradition. The museum collections of Jewish clothing show especially clearly that diaspora communities often wore clothing similar to that of their neighbours. The difference was created not necessarily by the overall silhouette but by a single detail, the way of covering the head, an ornament, a colour or the context of use.

The Jewish Museum exhibition "Veiled Meanings: Fashioning Jewish Dress" stressed the diversity of Jewish communities and the extent to which their clothing depended on the surrounding culture. This shatters the idea of religious clothing as a closed, unchanging system. Tradition is preserved not only through repetition but through adaptation: a foreign form may be appropriated by a community and filled with its own meaning.

In the Ottoman Empire clothing could historically designate religious and estate belonging. The state reforms of the nineteenth century, by contrast, sought partially to standardise the external appearance of subjects. Museum studies of late Ottoman clothing show how European forms — the fitted jacket, the skirt, the white wedding dress — gradually coexisted with local fabrics, adornments and religious customs. The transition to "European fashion" was not a one-off renunciation of identity; it created hybrid ensembles.

The Sikh tradition shows a different type of visibility. The turban can be at once a religious practice, the protection of uncut hair, a sign of dignity and a public identity. Historical depictions of Akali warriors show the towering dastar bunga, on which steel quoits were placed. Here the headdress united spiritual belonging, a military function and visual monumentality.

5. Ritual, power and ceremonial form

Ritual turns clothing into an instrument of transition. A person puts on a special object not only because it is beautiful or costly but because the clothing makes him a participant in a given action. The wedding costume marks a change in family status; monastic dress the renunciation of a previous social role; the vestment of a minister the right to perform a liturgical function; mourning clothing a publicly visible state of loss.

Ritual forms are distinguished by their stability. Their construction may be preserved for centuries, even when the fabrics, technique and decorative motifs change. This is precisely why religious clothing becomes a kind of archive: it holds old proportions and ways of draping that secular fashion long ago abandoned. When a designer turns to such an archive, he gains not simply a form but a sense of time, power and ceremony.

The luxury of religious vestments often seems to contradict the ideal of spirituality. But historically precious fabrics could be understood not as the personal consumption of the minister but as a gift to the deity, the temple or the community. Gold, silk and stones heightened the exclusivity of the ritual moment and created a distance between the everyday and the sacred. This same distance later proved attractive for the fashion show: the ceremonial slowness, the theatrical length, the metallic sheen and the monumental silhouette all build on the visual memory of the rite.

6. Christianity and the architecture of vestment

Christianity produced one of the most visible transfers of sacred codes into the secular language of design. The exhibition "Heavenly Bodies: Fashion and the Catholic Imagination" at The Metropolitan Museum of Art in 2018 gathered objects from the early twentieth century to the present and showed how designers used the form of the chasuble, the monastic habit, the mitre, the stole and the golden thread. John Galliano for Dior, Jean Paul Gaultier, Cristóbal Balenciaga, Alexander McQueen and others were presented within this frame. [1][2]

However, the exhibition was a curatorial statement of the museum, not proof that each named designer shared a Catholic worldview. Part of the participants stated that they worked with a visual, not a doctrinal, tradition. The article must therefore separate three layers: the documented use of liturgical and monastic form by a designer; the curatorial reading that connects the garment to the sacred; and the personal belief of the author, which may be absent, private or declared separately.

The monastic tradition influenced fashion through the logic of refusal. The robe, the hood, the coarse fabric, the absence of ornament and the deep dark colours externalised spiritual discipline. Contemporary fashion can borrow the monastic silhouette without its ascetic content, turning it into an elegant minimalism or a dramatic contrast to the festive. But the borrowing is not always neutral: the look that a designer calls "purified" the believer may perceive as a copy of a garment with a living ritual function.

7. Islam and the global system of modest fashion

Islamic dress culture is extraordinarily diverse. The Arab world, Iran, Turkey, South and Southeast Asia, Africa, Europe and the Americas each have their own history of covering, fabrics, silhouettes and the interaction of local tradition with the world fashion system.

Compulsory unveiling in some twentieth-century states produced a political backlash no less powerful than the compulsion to cover. In contemporary Iran the wearing of the hijab has been legally mandatory at various periods, while acts of public non-compliance have become a form of protest and a risk to personal safety. Clothing here is not a fashion choice; it is an instrument of state discipline and of resistance alike.

Muslim fashion is not reducible to the hijab. In Turkey, Indonesia, Malaysia, the Gulf countries, Europe and the US, the designers of modest fashion create clothing that satisfies the requirements of coveredness but competes aesthetically within the system of street style, luxury and influencers. This market creates new conflicts: when the abaya becomes a catwalk object, its religious function may be diluted; when a global brand launches a "modest collection", a question arises about who profits and who is represented in the creative team.

The relationship between Islam and fashion cannot be described through a single formula. In different regions and social strata, fashion can be an instrument of piety, a site of generational conflict, a means of economic independence, a political gesture and a form of aesthetic pleasure. All these meanings can coexist within one wardrobe and even within one garment.

8. Judaism: diaspora, law and adaptation

Jewish clothing has historically been shaped by the interplay of religious prescription, the surroundings of the diaspora and local fashion. The covering of the head for men varies from the kippah to the shtreimel; the rules for women include the covering of the hair after marriage, with different communities accepting a wig, a scarf, a hat or a turban in different periods. But the same object — a head covering — may be a sign of piety, a communal marker, a fashion accessory or a hybrid object, especially when it meets the contemporary design of colour and material.

The Jewish Museum's collections stress that diaspora communities often wore clothing similar to that of their neighbours, adding a detail, a distinctive stitch or a specific way of draping. The preservation of tradition takes place through adaptation, not only through conservation. The law — halakha — at once limits and creates: the prohibition on mixing wool and linen, for example, gave rise to a system of fabric inspection and the certification of materials. [7][8]

9. South and East Asia: fabric as a sacred object

In the Buddhist tradition the monastic robe traces its origin to patchwork, the reuse of discarded cloth and the idea of non-attachment. The kesa or kashaya was historically assembled from pieces of fabric, often donated; colour and the number of panels marked the grade, the tradition and the occasion. [12][13]

The robe is at once an object of discipline and of aesthetics. The straight cut, the large flat surfaces, the rhythm of the pleats and the contrast of the inner and outer side of the fabric influenced the language of Japanese designers — Issey Miyake, Yohji Yamamoto, Rei Kawakubo — who worked with wrapping, flatness and the distance between body and clothing. Here the archive does not supply a decorative motif; it gives a different construction of the relation of the person to the garment.

In India the sacred and the secular in textiles are historically closely interwoven. The V&A notes the use of fabrics in worship, at court and in trade, and the influence of Indian textile heritage on contemporary fashion. Gold thread, vegetable dye, block-printing, weaving and embroidery carry meanings whose attribution requires naming the region, the community and the function of the object. [14]

The turban in South Asia cannot be described as belonging to a single religion. It has an independent history of form, region, status and custom in Sikh, Hindu and Muslim communities alike.

10. When the sacred code enters haute couture

A sacred code can enter fashion through several paths. The designer may belong to a tradition himself and translate it into a contemporary cut and material. He may work with the archive of a country in which religion was part of the visual landscape. He may study monastic forms, liturgical gold, a relic, a crown or a painted icon and use them as a source of proportion, surface or ceremony. He may also turn to the sacred for provocation, the attraction of attention or the exploitation of a conflict.

The difference between these scenarios is not always visible in the final image. An object that for one viewer is a respectful homage may be for another an inadmissible appropriation or a cynical commercial gesture. The designer's statement helps to understand the intention but cannot dictate the perception. Especially in the case of borrowed traditions, the voice of the bearer of the culture is important — not as a veto but as a source of context without which the assessment is incomplete.

The exhibition "Heavenly Bodies" showed how strong the viewer's desire is to see design as a continuation of the sacred. But museums are interpretive machines. They select objects, build routes, place mannequins in poses and link medieval art with runway photographs. This construction is a legitimate form of knowledge, yet it must be recognised as a construction. [1][2][3][4]

11. Appropriation, commercialisation and the conflict of meanings

When a sacred code becomes a commercial product, several things happen simultaneously. The symbol is separated from the community, the ritual and the sanction that gave it its original function. It becomes reproducible through replication, scale, material and price. It enters a market where the value is determined not by spiritual significance but by scarcity, the brand and the fashion calendar.

Not every borrowing is appropriation. The boundary depends on concrete factors:

Is an object with a living sacral function being used, or is the motif historical?

Who creates the work, and what is his connection to the tradition?

Is the source named and the meaning explained?

Do the bearers of the culture or the craftsmen take part in the production, and do they receive fair recompense?

Has the meaning been altered in a way the community considers degrading?

Is the symbol being used only for shock and sale?

Is there a distinction between a museum interpretation, an artistic statement and a mass commodity?

In the religious sphere the conflict is especially sensitive because the symbol may be perceived not as cultural property but as an object of living faith. Its meaning is not exhausted by art history. An image that for a designer is a form may for another person be an object of prayer or of prohibition.

At the same time no single organisation can be given the right to speak on behalf of all believers. Reactions within any tradition differ. An editorially correct article must therefore attribute the assessments: not "the collection offended Christians" but "particular religious representatives and critics called the use of the symbol offensive", with an indication of the source, the date and the context.

12. Conclusion. Religion has not vanished from fashion — it has changed form

Contemporary fashion often describes itself as a secular and individualistic system. Yet its visual language carries a memory of religious forms. The long processional garment, the closed monastic volume, the gold of the altar, black mourning, the white wedding costume, the covered head, the amulet, the cross and sacred calligraphy all continue to take part in how we read the silhouette.

Religion influenced fashion not through a single direction but through a network of relations. It restricted and inspired; it preserved forms and produced novelties; it demanded modesty and created an exclusive luxury; it erased individuality and gave a community a recognisable image. The wearers of religious clothing were never merely passive executors of rules: they interpreted the norm, chose the material, the colour, the degree of decoration and the way of joining faith with modernity.

When the sacred code passes into fashion, it changes function but does not become empty. Even detached from its theological core, it carries the memory of power, belonging, prohibition, protection, death and hope. It is precisely this residual force that makes religious images so attractive to designers — and so sensitive for society.

The main conclusion: fashion does not simply borrow religious symbols. It constantly reorders the boundary between the body and the norm, personal freedom and the community, material beauty and spiritual meaning.

Appendix B. Glossary and neutral objects

Religious clothing. Clothing or an accessory whose function, rules of wearing or meaning are tied to religious practice, office, ritual or identity.

Modest dress. Clothing that meets given norms of coveredness, opacity, freedom of silhouette and restraint; the criteria depend on the tradition and the community.

Modest fashion. A commercial and design category of contemporary clothing that combines fashionability with a heightened covering of the body. It is not equal to a single religion.

Liturgical vestments. Special clothing used by clergy during a service.

Monastic dress. Clothing of members of monastic communities, usually marking belonging, discipline and distance from secular life.

Hijab. In a wide religious sense, a principle or practice of modesty; in contemporary everyday speech it often designates a women's headscarf covering the hair and neck.

Abaya. A loose outer garment common in a number of Arab societies; the specific form and practice of wearing vary.

Chador. A large semicircular covering wrapped around the body and head; linked above all to the Iranian context.

Niqab. A face covering that leaves the eyes visible; the ways of combining it with other clothing vary.

Kesa / kashaya. A Buddhist monastic robe, historically linked to patchwork construction and the idea of non-attachment.

Turban. A way of wrapping fabric around the head; it has many regional, social and religious forms and does not belong to a single tradition.

Dastar. A Sikh turban; a significant element of religious identity for many Sikhs.

Talismanic textile. A fabric or garment to which a protective force is attributed through text, ornament, material or ritual use.

Ritual clothing. Clothing used in a specific rite or transitional state: wedding, mourning, initiation, pilgrimage, etc.

Sumptuary laws. Legal or social norms restricting the use of certain fabrics, colours, furs, adornments or silhouettes by class, office, gender or group.

Cultural appropriation. A contested form of borrowing in which elements of a culture are used outside their context, often amid a power imbalance, without recognition, participation or benefit for their bearers.

Attribution. An explicit indication of the source of an idea, object, image, tradition or interpretation.

Sacred code. A visual element that is read as tied to the sacral: a symbol, a colour, a form, a material, a text or a ritual composition.
