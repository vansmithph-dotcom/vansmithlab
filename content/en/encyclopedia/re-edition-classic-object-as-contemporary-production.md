# Re-edition — the classic object as contemporary production

*A re-edition creates a new legitimate object rather than a copy: the difference lies in rights and in production documentation.*

## Editorial thesis

Re-edition matters as a case in which authenticity is secured not by an object’s age but by continuity of rights and recovered technology.

## Reader question

How does an example of a historical model produced today differ from a counterfeit, if they look identical?

## Short answer

By the declaration of origin: a rights holder produces a new object and says so, whereas a counterfeit asserts an origin it does not have.

## 1. Definition and boundaries

A re-edition is the production of a historical model by the rights holder from recovered or preserved documentation. It does not pretend to be an original and is therefore an object in its own right.

## 2. Four different things

Original, re-edition, unlicensed replica, and counterfeit form four distinct statuses. What separates them is not resemblance but the combination of rights and declaration.

## 3. Rights as the foundation

The ability to produce a model belongs to whoever holds rights, inherited or acquired. It is legal continuity, not technical accuracy, that makes production legitimate.

## 4. Term of protection

Rights in an industrial design are finite, and on expiry a model may be produced by anyone. Hence a market of lawful but unauthorised versions, legally clean and stripped of provenance.

## 5. Recovering tooling

Moulds and dies rarely survive decades. A re-edition usually requires new tooling made from drawings or from measuring a surviving example.

## 6. Measurement as reconstruction

When documentation is lost, an example is measured and the model reconstructed. The result is inevitably an interpretation: measurement records one particular example with all its deviations.

## 7. Which example is the standard

Early and late runs of one model differ. Choosing which to measure is an editorial decision determining which version of the history will be reproduced.

## 8. Material substitution

Some original materials are prohibited or unavailable. Substitution is unavoidable and changes an object’s weight, behaviour, and ageing while the silhouette is preserved.

## 9. Regulatory requirements

Contemporary standards for flammability, stability, and safety are frequently not met by a historical construction. A re-edition must depart from the original in order to be lawful.

## 10. Lost technologies

Some operations are no longer performed: machines, skills, or permitted compounds have disappeared. Certain objects are fundamentally irreproducible, and this is a technical rather than a legal problem.

## 11. Resizing for the contemporary body

Anthropometry has shifted, and historical proportions frequently feel tight. Manufacturers choose between accuracy and usability, and the choice is rarely stated publicly.

## 12. Marking the edition

Good practice is a stamp with date and number distinguishing a contemporary example from a historical one. It is the technical foundation of the entire authenticity market.

## 13. Effect on the collectors’ market

A re-edition usually raises interest in the original while complicating its identification. Both effects appear at once and pull in opposite directions.

## 14. The archive as production asset

A company that preserved drawings and samples can manufacture history. The archive here is not memory but a working department.

## 15. Selecting what is reissued

Models that sell are returned, rather than those that describe an era most accurately. A canon built on re-editions is systematically skewed toward the commercially successful.

## 16. Colour as a contemporary decision

Historical models are frequently reissued in palettes that did not exist in the author’s lifetime. This is an honest continuation of production and simultaneously a distortion of historical evidence.

## 17. Heirs’ consent

Beyond rights lies the question of the author’s intent. Heirs and foundations participate in decisions, and their position does not always coincide with legal possibility.

## 18. First turn: the archive became merchandise

Recognising that history sells turned a collection of samples into a source of revenue. This changed manufacturers’ relations with their own past.

## 19. Second turn: authenticity became a document

A certificate, a number, and a register entry replaced age as the mark of authenticity. Credibility moved from the object into paperwork.

## 20. Third turn: expiry opened the market

The end of protection on a number of landmark models produced lawful parallel manufacture. The dispute moved from law into provenance and quality.

## 21. What is usually misunderstood

The error is to treat a re-edition as a copy. Legally and industrially it is a new object; what makes something a copy is not its manufacture but a false claim of origin.

## 22. Counterargument

The objection is cultural: re-editions preserve a canon and occupy space that could go to new authors. A manufacturer chooses a safe past over a risky present.

## 23. Where the counterargument holds

It holds as a description of portfolios: re-editions account for such a share of revenue at some companies that new development is funded on a residual basis.

## 24. Where it fails

It fails as an objection to the practice itself. Continuous production preserves technologies and skills that would otherwise be lost irrecoverably.

## 25. Link to the museum

Institution and manufacturer present the same model differently: the museum shows an example with a history, the factory sells a working object. Both statements are true and do not coincide.

## 26. Link to citation

Re-edition relates to citation as performance relates to reference: both are legitimate when the source is named and both become appropriation when it is concealed.

## 27. Link to sustainability

A long-lived model with available parts is the opposite of a seasonal range. Re-edition has incidentally proved one of the industry’s most sustainable practices.

## 28. Restoration versus re-edition

Restoring a particular example and producing a new batch solve different problems. The first preserves evidence, the second continues manufacture.

## 29. Current state

The practice has become routine and commercially significant, and distinguishing versions has become specialist knowledge. The market now requires expertise where a glance once sufficed.

## 30. How to read the subject today

Ask three things: who holds the rights, from what documentation it was made, and how a contemporary example is marked. The answers separate continued manufacture from trade in resemblance.
