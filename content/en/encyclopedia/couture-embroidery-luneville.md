# Couture Embroidery

*The Lunéville hook and the French ateliers*

> In couture, embroidery does not decorate a finished surface: it designs light, weight, relief, and movement together with the garment's construction.

## Editorial thesis

Couture embroidery is not a decorative layer added to a finished garment but a system in which image, weight, light, tactility, and construction are designed together. The Lunéville hook accelerated the attachment of beads and sequins with chain stitch, yet quality still depends on drawing, sampling, fabric tension, material distribution, and collective atelier labour.[^clm_001]

## Reader question

Why can two dresses carrying the same number of sequins differ radically in quality, movement, light, and price?

## Short answer

In couture embroidery, what matters is not only the material but how it is fixed, stitch direction, density, weight, the reverse, and the relationship to garment construction. Lunéville uses fabric stretched on a frame and a small hook working from the reverse to create chain stitch and secure beads, pearls, and sequins.[^clm_001][^clm_002]

French maisons d'art divide work among drawing, sampling, fabric preparation, embroidery, mounting, and quality control. Lesage, Atelier Montex, and other specialist houses serve haute couture and ready-to-wear while preserving hand techniques and introducing new materials and processes.[^clm_003][^clm_006]

## 1. Couture embroidery as a system

Couture embroidery is designed with the silhouette. Material, density, and motif direction must account for bodily movement, seams, and garment weight.

## 2. The history of Lunéville

The Met links the development of Lunéville embroidery, also called tambour beading, to Louis Ferry-Bonnechaux's invention in the mid-1860s.[^clm_001]

## 3. The frame

Fabric is stretched on a wooden frame so that surface tension remains stable. Weak tension distorts chain stitch; excessive tension can deform the ground.

## 4. Working from the reverse

The hook usually works from the reverse while beads and sequins are fed on the face. The embroiderer must sense both sides at once.[^clm_002]

## 5. Chain stitch

The continuous chain is the basis of the technique. It secures elements quickly but demands even rhythm and constant thread control.

## 6. The Lunéville hook

A fine hook passes through the cloth, catches the working thread, and forms a loop. Rotation determines whether the chain holds or unravels.

## 7. Beads on the thread

Beads are pre-strung on the working thread and fed one at a time. Speed depends on coordination between the hand below the frame and the hand above.

## 8. Sequins

Sequins may lie flat, overlap, stand vertically, or hang freely. Direction determines reflection and movement.

## 9. Pearls and large elements

Heavy elements often need additional needle securing. Lunéville is efficient, but it does not replace every type of hand embroidery.

## 10. Needle and hook

Needle embroidery and Lunéville complement one another. le19M distinguishes needle, crochet, and hand-guided machine embroidery.[^clm_002]

## 11. Cornely

Atelier Montex also specialises in Cornely, a hand-guided embroidery machine capable of working several threads and producing dimensional textures.[^clm_007]

## 12. The design

Before embroidery, a dessin translates the designer's idea into an executable motif, accounting for scale, density, and material direction.

## 13. The sampler

le19M states that a sampler first creates a small prototype interpreting the client's artistic instructions.[^clm_002]

## 14. Pouncing

An outline may be transferred through pricked tracing paper and powder passing through the holes, a process shown by CHANEL in Atelier Montex.[^clm_006]

## 15. The ground fabric

Organza, tulle, silk, crepe, wool, leather, and synthetics respond differently to puncture, weight, and tension.

## 16. Transparent organza

Organza exposes the internal logic of stitches and can make decoration appear suspended, but reverse-side errors become highly visible.

## 17. Tulle

Tulle visually dissolves the ground but its open cells demand precise placement of every puncture.

## 18. Dense cloth

Wool, leather, and dense jacquard can carry heavy decoration but resist the hook and increase total weight.

## 19. Thread

Cotton, silk, polyester, metallic thread, and monofilament differ in strength, visibility, and response to light.

## 20. Materials beyond tradition

Contemporary couture embroidery uses plastic, resin, metal, wood, film, and found matter. The Met notes that new synthetics expanded surface embellishment.[^clm_001]

## 21. Motif density

High density can turn cloth into an almost rigid surface. Sparse embroidery retains movement and allows the ground to participate.

## 22. Weight

Every bead adds mass. Weight must be distributed or the garment stretches, loses balance, and loads the shoulders.

## 23. Light

Sequins reflect directionally, beads fragment light, pearls produce a soft glow, and metallic thread creates linear highlights.

## 24. Colour

Colour is formed by material, reflection, and shadow. The same sequin can read differently on flat and curved areas.

## 25. Relief

Padding, cords, dimensional pieces, and layered application create raised embroidery, changing both light and fabric mechanics.

## 26. Direction

Identical sequins laid in different directions create different light fields. Direction can model bodily volume.

## 27. Gradient

Gradients can shift colour, size, density, and material—not merely replace one hue with another.

## 28. Negative space

Empty areas are as important as filled ones. They preserve movement and create pauses inside complex surfaces.

## 29. Seam and motif

A motif must intentionally cross a seam or stop before it. Accidental interruption destroys the image.

## 30. Embroidery on flat pieces

Pieces are often embroidered before final assembly while flat enough to stretch on a frame.

## 31. Embroidery after assembly

Some elements are added after assembly to hide joins, restore continuity, or work across volume.

## 32. Mounting

Finished embroidery is removed from the frame, stabilised, and integrated with garment construction. Mounting is a distinct stage.

## 33. Lesage

Maison Lesage traces its modern history to 1924, creates embroidery for haute couture, ready-to-wear, and accessories, and joined CHANEL's Métiers d'art in 2002.[^clm_005]

## 34. École Lesage

École Lesage opened in 1992 at François Lesage's initiative. le19M reports more than 4,000 students and training for beginners and professionals.[^clm_004]

## 35. The sample collection

Lesage preserves the world's largest collection of embroidery samples, an archive of techniques, materials, and visual language.[^clm_003]

## 36. Atelier Montex

Atelier Montex specialises in needle embroidery, Lunéville crochet, and Cornely, combining historic techniques with experimentation.[^clm_007]

## 37. le19M

le19M, initiated by CHANEL and opened in Paris in 2021, brings together 11 maisons d'art and functions of production, transmission, and public culture.[^clm_006]

## 38. Schiaparelli and Lesage

In the 1930s Schiaparelli and Lesage created surreal and thematic surfaces. The Met credits House of Lesage for the embroidery of her 1937 Zodiac jacket.[^clm_008]

## 39. Yves Saint Laurent and Lesage

Yves Saint Laurent used embroidery to translate painting and decorative art into dress. The Met links Lesage to his 1968–69 black dress and 1988 Irises.[^clm_009]

## 40. Irises

The 1988 Irises jacket required more than 600 hours, 250 metres of ribbon, 200,000 beads, and 250,000 sequins in 22 colours; nine women worked on separate sections.[^clm_010]

## 41. Embroidery and painting

In Irises, materials do not copy painting literally: sequins, beads, and ribbon translate Van Gogh's brushwork, colour, and impasto into textile structure.

## 42. Balmain and weight balance

In a 2001 Balmain dress, the direction of Lesage embroidery helped balance light organza against heavy crystal beading and control the hem's fluted fall.[^clm_012]

## 43. Collective authorship

The final object is produced by designer, embroidery designer, sampler, embroiderers, pattern cutters, seamstresses, and mounting specialists. Authorship is distributed.

## 44. Time and price

Price reflects not only hours but drawing complexity, rare materials, number of prototypes, risk of error, and team expertise.

## 45. How to photograph embroidery

At least two scales are needed: full view for silhouette and macro for stitch. Side light reveals relief; controlled frontal light clarifies colour and sheen.

## 46. Movement in the frame

Sequins and hanging elements need movement because their light effect unfolds over time. Total freeze can conceal the mechanics.

## 47. What a designer should analyse

Check ground cloth, weight, element size, density, load points, seams, assembly order, and access to the reverse.

## 48. What an art director should analyse

Complex embroidery is already the principal image. Background, jewellery, and props should reinforce one thesis rather than compete.

## 49. The ethics of craft

Couture embroidery should not be described as magical handwork while concealing names, conditions, and collective expertise.

## 50. Conclusion

Couture embroidery turns garment surface into a controlled system of light, weight, relief, and image. The Lunéville hook accelerates execution, but quality comes from drawing, sampling, materials, mounting, and collective atelier knowledge.
