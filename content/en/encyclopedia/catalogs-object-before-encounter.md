# Catalogues — the object before the encounter

*A catalogue forms the expectation of a thing before the thing is seen, and therefore designs perception on equal terms with the object.*

## Editorial thesis

The catalogue matters as an object’s first carrier: most things are known through image and description rather than experience, and it is the catalogue that sets that image.

## Reader question

What does a catalogue do beyond listing what is available to buy?

## Short answer

It standardises description, sets a unit of comparison, and turns heterogeneous objects into comparable entries — creating a market rather than merely serving one.

## 1. Definition and boundaries

A catalogue is an ordered list of objects with descriptions, images, and attributes sufficient for ordering without inspection. The key condition is sufficiency: description must substitute for presence.

## 2. The trade catalogue as a genre

Nineteenth-century industrial catalogues presented factory ranges and are today a principal source for the history of manufacture. They document what did not survive in objects.

## 3. The article number as an invention

Assigning an object a stable number made it orderable at a distance and repeatably. The article number made a thing addressable, and this preceded mass production rather than following it.

## 4. Standardising description

For objects to be compared they must be described identically: the same fields, units, and order. That editorial discipline is a catalogue’s principal content.

## 5. Mail order

Selling by catalogue took a range beyond the city and served remote districts. The catalogue was an infrastructure of access long before electronic commerce.

## 6. The image as a promise

A drawing, and later a photograph, sets an expectation. The gap between image and delivered object is the genre’s persistent problem and produced the rules of return.

## 7. From drawing to photograph

Engraving allowed an object to be shown idealised and explained; photography brought credibility and simultaneously required staging. The change of technique changed the nature of the promise.

## 8. The room setting

Showing an object in a setting sells a scenario of living rather than a product. The device shifts the decision from whether I need this thing to whether I want to live like that.

## 9. The furniture company catalogue

Mid-century furniture manufacturers’ indexes did double work: they served ordering and asserted the range’s status as a collection. The catalogue here is closer to a museum publication than to a price list.

## 10. The catalogue as an annual publication

A regular mass catalogue worked like a magazine about the home: it appeared on schedule, set a season, and shaped ideas of domestic normality far beyond its buyers.

## 11. Print run as cultural influence

A catalogue printed in tens of millions distributes a visual norm more widely than any museum. Its influence comes from volume rather than authority.

## 12. Order as argument

The sequence of sections asserts a hierarchy: what is shown first, what is relegated to the back. A catalogue’s contents page is an editorial statement.

## 13. Absence as decision

What is not in a catalogue defines a range as much as what is. Excluding an entry is the compiler’s strongest and least visible action.

## 14. The specification as a genre

For a professional buyer a catalogue is a technical document: dimensions, loads, materials, lead times. Here the image is secondary and the table primary.

## 15. The exhibition catalogue

A museum catalogue records a display after it is dismantled. It is the catalogue, not the gallery, that gives an exhibition long life and citability.

## 16. The catalogue as archive

Surviving catalogues allow objects to be dated and manufacturers identified. For design history this is a more reliable source than recollection.

## 17. Price as part of description

Stating a price places an object in a comparable series and makes economic judgement possible. Catalogues without prices serve a different purpose — reputation rather than transaction.

## 18. First turn: ordering at a distance

Buying the unseen required trust in description. From this came the return guarantee and the seller’s reputation as a separate asset.

## 19. Second turn: the catalogue became a publication

An editorial component — texts, narratives, room settings — turned a list into media. The catalogue began producing desire rather than merely serving choice.

## 20. Third turn: the list became a database

Moving a range into a filtered interface destroyed order and contents. The reader stopped passing through a catalogue whole, and editorial work shifted to the algorithm.

## 21. What is usually misunderstood

The error is to treat a catalogue as a reflection of a range. It is that range’s construction: how entries are described and ordered determines what will be noticed at all.

## 22. Counterargument

The objection is practical: in the age of search a catalogue is redundant, since a buyer arrives with a query and needs nobody else’s ordering.

## 23. Where the counterargument holds

It holds when an object is known in advance. For repeat purchase of a known item a list is genuinely unnecessary.

## 24. Where it fails

It fails when an object is unknown. Search finds only what one can name; a catalogue shows what one did not suspect existed.

## 25. Link to photography

Catalogue photography developed its own genre: even light, neutral ground, a recognisable angle. That language became the standard image of an object everywhere.

## 26. Link to typography

The dense tabular layout of a catalogue is a difficult typographic problem: much data, little space, mandatory comparability between rows. Here the grid works at its limit.

## 27. Link to logistics

A catalogue cannot promise what is not supplied. Range, lead times, and delivery geography constrain a list before editorial selection begins.

## 28. The end of print editions

Major companies abandoning the printed catalogue in the early 2020s marked the audience’s move to digital channels. With the paper went the prescribed order of viewing.

## 29. Current state

The catalogue’s function has split into search, feed, and recommendation. The task is unchanged — to present an object before the encounter — but is performed without a single document and without a visible author.

## 30. How to read the subject today

Look at order and at omissions. What is shown first and what is absent entirely are the two questions that expose the editorial position of any list, including an algorithmic one.
